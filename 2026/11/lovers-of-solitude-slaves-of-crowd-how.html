How a Shy Grasshopper Becomes a Locust Swarm

ArticlesEnglishlang-enScience
November 16, 2026Written by बासभ
Share
New pieces by email, Nepali and English.Subscribe

Many will remember the locust swarms that arrived during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic. Not all grasshoppers are locusts, but every locust was once a grasshopper.

Crowded together, bumping into one another until their serotonin surges, they become locusts, and their all-devouring march to conquer the world is as unstable as it is destructive. A locust swarm is wiped out within a few months, and the survivors take generations to revert to grasshoppers.

As easy as it is for a calm grasshopper to become a locust, returning to being a grasshopper once it has become a locust is far harder.

Grasshopper and locust (from the internet).

In short

  • Locusts are not a separate species: only about a dozen of the world’s roughly 7,000 grasshopper species can turn into locusts.
  • When food runs short and grasshoppers crowd together, their hind legs keep bumping, serotonin surges, and their behavior starts changing within hours.
  • The change is epigenetic, not a mutation: only which part of the DNA gets read changes.
  • A locust lives barely three months; without rain to lay eggs, a swarm collapses on its own.
  • The 2020 swarms reached 55 districts of Nepal but died in the cold hills; in 1996, swarms wiped out crops in Chitwan, Makwanpur, Mahottari and Bara.

Moses, Locusts and the Bible’s Eighth Plague

The Hebrew Bible, written about three thousand years ago, recounts the story of ten plagues that struck Egypt. The Hebrew Bible is Judaism's primary scripture, and its second book, Exodus, tells the story of Moses. The same story of Exodus appears in the first part of the Christian Bible, the Old Testament.

According to the story, Moses worked in the Egyptian palace as an aide to the Pharaoh. One day, he learns that he is not an Egyptian at all but a descendant of the Israelites brought there as slaves. Moses pleads with the Pharaoh to free all the Israelites enslaved in Egypt. But the Pharaoh doesn’t listen; instead, he throws Moses out of the palace.

Moses then rebels against Egypt and frees all the Israelites who had been brought there as slaves. He leads them across the ‘Red Sea’ and through the Arabian Peninsula to Israel. That is the central story of Exodus.

But no story in the world is complete without a revenge.

Before crossing the sea, Moses raises his hand and begs God to punish them all: the rulers of Egypt who enslaved them and the people who tormented them. God hears Moses’s plea and inflicts Egypt with ten plagues.

The river’s water turned to blood. A flood of frogs came, a plague struck the livestock, and hail fell. The eighth plague was locusts.

A wind blew from the east for a day and a night. The next morning, that wind brought such a swarm of locusts that the ground disappeared from view and darkness fell in broad daylight. There was uproar everywhere; every crop was destroyed, and not even the wheat spared by the hail remained.

A locust swarm blotting out the sky (from the internet).

A few days later, the wind shifted, and a west wind swept the locusts away and dropped them into the eastern sea. In the story of Exodus, the locust plague in Egypt ended this way, on its own.

Writing had begun in Egypt some fifteen hundred years before the story of Exodus was written in the Bible. But nowhere in Egypt’s records is a locust plague described. Therefore, this locust story in Exodus should be called a story, not history.

Even so, we can’t say for certain that the Egyptians never saw locusts. Here and there, pictures of grasshoppers are painted in the tombs where they laid their ‘mummies.’ From the pictures alone, it is impossible to tell whether they depict grasshoppers or locusts.

Not all grasshoppers are locusts, but every locust was once a grasshopper.

Today I’ve set out to write about the strange relationship between grasshoppers and locusts. I don’t know how long this piece will be. But if you read to the end, you will find the locust’s whole story.

The First Record of Locusts in History

If we look for locusts not only in stories but also in real history, we have to travel to present-day Iraq, some six hundred years after the Hebrew Bible was written. In the seventh century BCE, Ashurbanipal was king of Assyria (in northern Iraq).

We can still see a scene carved on the wall of his palace from that time.

Ashurbanipal’s palace, the first image of a locust (British Museum, c. 640 BCE).

The king reclines in his garden, drinking wine, with the queen seated beside him. Around them lies a garden of date palms and grapevines. In the garden stands a tree: from one branch hangs the head of the king of Elam (Iran), and on another branch is the image of a locust. Nearby, a bird watches the locust, as if waiting to eat it.

Ashurbanipal used to call Elam’s army ‘a thick swarm of locusts.’ It is thought that the lone locust in the tree was meant to represent the defeated enemy’s army.

In Assyria, investigating and writing about locusts had begun even before Ashurbanipal’s time. Notes recording research on the different stages of locusts, from the palace at Nineveh, appear on tablets dating to a few decades before Ashurbanipal.

Nearly three hundred years after Ashurbanipal, Aristotle wrote about the locust’s life cycle in his book on animals. That book, which classified the locust as a species of its own, remained our main source on locusts for a long time.

The Locust’s Journey to Nepal

Sometimes Egypt. Sometimes Iraq and Assyria. Sometimes Aristotle. And all of it was three thousand years ago. Locusts may seem far away to us, but in our own Nepal, there is a history of locust swarms wiping out crops several times.

Many will still remember locust swarms entering Nepal during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic. Originating in the Arabian Desert and moving through India, the first swarms entered Nepal on the morning of June 27 at three locations: Rupandehi, Bara, and Sarlahi. More swarms arrived the next day and the day after. On July 12, another swarm entered through Dang and reached as far as Syangja via Pyuthan, Gulmi, and Baglung. On July 16, more arrived through Banke and Kanchanpur, and the swarms that entered through Banke reached as far as Jajarkot via Surkhet and Dailekh.

At that time, locust swarms were seen in 55 of the country’s districts. But the swarms didn’t do much damage to crops. That’s because they headed straight for Nepal’s hills, where the cold air proved deadly to them.

That time, Nepal’s hills wiped out the swarms. But we can’t always count on the hills.

Exactly twenty-four years before the COVID-era swarms, in 1996, locusts entered Nepal through Chitwan. The swarms that came then destroyed nearly 80 percent of the crops in Chitwan, Makwanpur, Mahottari, and Bara. That is the worst destruction locusts have caused in Nepal.

The earliest recorded locust swarms and damage in Nepal’s history date to 1962. At that time, crops in the districts around Kathmandu are said to have been badly damaged. Over the five years from 1926 to 1930, locusts are said to have caused about one hundred million pounds of damage in India. Some sources report that Juddha Shumsher received news at that time of locusts arriving in Nepal’s Tarai.

All three times, the locusts that came to Nepal came from the Arabian Desert. If the locusts in the Exodus story were real, they too must have come from there. Where they ended up depended on the wind: when the east wind blew, they reached Nepal, and when the west wind blew, they reached Egypt.

All the locusts of Asia, Europe, and Africa come from the vast desert belt stretching from West Africa to India. But it would be wrong to think of the locust as a separate species. The calm grasshoppers living all around us are the very locusts that brought the Bible’s plague.

Now let me explain in detail how a grasshopper turns into a locust.

A locust is not a new species or some strange insect.

The Green Insect in the Grass

We have all seen grasshoppers everywhere: on the ridges between rice fields, in grassy meadows, and along the edges of farm plots. Green, the color of grass.

The green insect that lives alone in the grass (from the internet).

Touch one, and it jumps, lands a little way off, and settles again. Catch one and hold it in your hand, and it kicks its legs and, frightened, soon spits a brown juice from its mouth.

A grasshopper does no harm to anyone. It doesn’t destroy anyone’s field. Instead, it becomes food for birds, lizards, and frogs.

It prefers to live alone. If it meets another grasshopper, it moves away. In the morning, it basks in the sun, eats leaves, and hides again.

About 7,000 species of grasshoppers exist worldwide. Most of them spend their lives as loners, meaning they remain grasshoppers forever. They spend their whole lives trying not to be seen, which is why most are the same green as grass. In scientific terms, this solitude-loving nature is called the solitarious phase.

But there are about a dozen grasshopper species that can abandon their solitary nature entirely and enter an aggressive form: the gregarious phase. In other words, they can stop being grasshoppers and become locusts.

A locust is not a new species or some strange insect. In grasshoppers that can become locusts, the DNA is like two different sections of the same book. Read only the grasshopper section of the DNA book, and they stay grasshoppers forever; read the locust section, and they can turn into locusts.

Every Locust Is a Grasshopper, Not Every Grasshopper Is a Locust

When rain falls in the desert, plants sprout everywhere and food is plentiful. Grasshoppers lay eggs, the young grow up, and their numbers rise. While food is plentiful, they follow the desert’s greenery and continue living solitary lives.

And then the rain stops.

The green that was everywhere now survives only in a few patches: here beside a gully, there on damp ground. With food left in only a few places, the grasshoppers start gathering there as well, in search of it.

What we must not forget here is that the grasshoppers didn’t gather in one place because their numbers grew; they were forced together because food was scarce.

Grasshoppers forced together by food shortage (from the internet).

Creatures that prefer to live alone are now forced to crowd together, and a crowd of grasshoppers forms. As they shift and jump, their long hind legs bump into others’ legs. Grabbing a leaf means jostling with another. Amid the shoving and pushing, their legs keep knocking together repeatedly.

It is this very knocking of legs that turns grasshoppers into locusts.

The grasshoppers didn’t gather in one place because their numbers grew; they were forced together because food ran short.

As their long hind legs keep bumping into one another, changes begin inside their bodies. Our bodies have this hormone, too: serotonin. When we are happy, sad, or excited, our bodies make more of it.

The bumping of grasshoppers’ legs multiplies the serotonin in their bodies many times over. What serotonin does in our bodies is still debated, but there is no doubt about its role in a grasshopper.

The Changes That Follow a Serotonin Surge

Within a few hours of serotonin rising in a grasshopper’s body, change begins. Its old habit of living alone starts to shut down completely. In its place, new genes that teach it to live in swarms, become aggressive, and fly nonstop begin to switch on.

Within a few days, the body begins to change.

The green grasshopper molts. The grass-green color disappears, and in its place appear deep yellow, liver-red, and black. It no longer needs to hide, so it no longer needs those colors.

Its wings grow longer, its muscles stronger, and even its brain grows larger. With a long journey ahead, it must move in a crowd, watch what others do, and make more decisions.

These insects are no longer solitary grasshoppers. They have just one purpose now: to advance together and devour whatever lies ahead.

At first, they march across the ground in bands; once their wings grow and they take off in swarms, they become thesame sky-blotting ‘locust swarm’ that brought darkness to Egypt three thousand years ago.

How Does a Serotonin Surge Turn a Grasshopper into a Locust?

Now the question arises: how can serotonin alone produce such an astonishing change in a grasshopper’s body?

To be honest, this tiny chemical’s role is only to carry the news of bumping legs to the cells. Serotonin doesn’t even enter the cells. It stops at the cell membrane, and once the cells recognize it, they begin other changes in the grasshopper’s body.

The serotonin that surges when a grasshopper’s legs bump settles on the cell membrane. There, a protein recognizes serotonin: a G-protein-coupled receptor. On the outside of the cell, the receptor’s mouth recognizes the extra serotonin in the body and binds it.

Once serotonin binds, the receptor’s engine inside the cell switches on. It then sends the news that serotonin has risen through messengers called cyclic AMP (cAMP). Just as a speaker turns up a microphone’s volume so everyone can hear, these messengers amplify the news of rising serotonin many times over inside the cell.

PKA, the Protein Serotonin Switches On

These messengers carry the serotonin signal to another protein, PKA. This PKA protein is the main culprit that turns a grasshopper into a locust. It works in two ways.

Its first job happens instantly, in the nerves. Within the first few minutes of PKA rising in a grasshopper’s body, it begins attaching phosphate to nerve-cell ion channels. Those channels, through which chemicals pass, begin to open. Once the channels loosen, signals travel faster through the nerve cells.

This is what triggers the first behavioral change as a grasshopper turns into a locust. Grasshoppers that haven’t yet become locusts become aggressive, must keep moving, and start seeking company.

Up to this point, nothing has changed in their DNA. All of this is just a simple adjustment to proteins that were already present.

But PKA’s work doesn’t end here.

A few hours of bumping legs is enough to turn a grasshopper into a locust. Turning a locust back into a grasshopper takes generations.

The Biological Change PKA Brings to the Grasshopper

PKA’s second job is the main one. A small piece of it travels to the cell’s center, the nucleus, and brings about biological change to the DNA.

In the book of DNA, closed pages start to open, and open pages start to close.

The small piece of PKA that reaches the nucleus attaches a phosphate to a protein there called CREB. CREB is a transcription factor. Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific sites on DNA and signal for the genes there to be read. Adding the phosphate should be understood as a signal to start work.

The most important change comes next. Interestingly, the new proteins produced once CREB opens the way carry both types of signals: to close open DNA and to keep closed DNA open. The genes that make a grasshopper now switch off, and the genes a locust needs begin to switch on.

Proteins called DNA methyltransferases switch off the genes needed to be a grasshopper. They add methyl groups to one of the four repeating letters of DNA (A, T, G, C): cytosine (C). Adding methyl groups makes the DNA tighter.

The more methyl groups are added to any part of the DNA, the more tightly that part winds up. The machinery needed for transcription (the process of making RNA from DNA) can’t attach to DNA that has wound up tight, so no RNA or protein is made.

This is one of the things CREB does when it receives the serotonin signal: within a few hours, the genes that the grasshopper’s solitary life depends on begin to switch off.

The genes for being a grasshopper and the genes for being a locust are in the same DNA. Whether it stays a grasshopper or becomes a locust depends on which part of the DNA is read and when. This is where CREB comes in again, switching on another protein called HAT (histone acetyltransferase).

HAT’s job is to attach acetyl groups to DNA. Adding acetyl groups does exactly the opposite of adding methyl groups. The more acetyl groups are added to DNA, the looser that part of the DNA becomes.

The biological process as a grasshopper becomes a locust (made with the help of Claude AI).

In this way, the grasshopper's genes are switched off and the locust's genes are switched on at the same time.

What we need to understand here is that even though the ability to become a locust is present from the start, it is impossible to remain a locust forever. That is why nature works to keep them in the grasshopper state.

A serotonin surge upsets nature’s effort. The padlock on the grasshopper section opens. The first, grasshopper section closes, and the book’s other, locust section begins to be read.

Another important point is that the change from grasshopper to locust is not genetic but epigenetic, shaped by the environment. In other words, no mutation occurs.

Normally, changes in methylation are not passed down from one generation to the next. That biological fact holds true when a locust lays its eggs: the young developing inside the egg develop just like grasshoppers.

But here the locust pulls off another marvel.

Along with her eggs, the mother locust leaves behind a foam-like substance. What exactly this foam is and how it works remain unknown. But because of this foam, the young are born already as aggressive as locusts.

The Locust’s Unstable Life

As terrifying as the swarm’s aggressive, all-devouring march is, it is equally unstable. Look at Nepal itself: the locusts seen in 55 districts in 2020 disappeared on their own within a few months.

A swarm of tens of millions needs a constant supply of food to keep going. An adult locust eats its own weight in food every day. But this boundless hunger becomes the main cause of their downfall.

To avoid starvation, once they have stripped one area of greenery, they are forced to keep flying in search of food. In the end, this great journey ends harshly. Most locusts die exhausted in mid-flight or without finding food along the way.

Such a way of life can never last; the swarm is always on the brink of hunger and crisis. But food shortage is only one reason for their downfall.

First of all, we need to understand that however aggressively they march, as if to wipe out the whole world, locusts are not immortal. Their life cycle is limited: they live only about three months.

In other words, any locust swarm ends within three months, no matter what. Even if no one sprays pesticides, even if the wind doesn’t change, even if food is plentiful. To keep going, locusts have to keep laying new eggs, and that is where their problem begins.

Female locusts can’t lay eggs just anywhere. They need moist sand to lay them. Once the young hatch, they need soft, green plants. Both of these come from one thing: rain.

Without rain, mother locusts can’t lay eggs. Even if they do, the eggs die in dry sand. Even if the young hatch, there is nothing to eat. So a swarm ends not because food runs out but because the next generation fails to survive. The old locusts age and die, and no one is born to take their place.

Another important point: a locust doesn’t fly wherever it likes; it flies with the wind. This usually works because the wind tends to blow toward places where it is raining, and there food is plentiful. In other words, riding the wind is not foolishness; it is a system.

But the system breaks down.

If the wind blows the wrong way, the whole swarm ends up over the sea. There is nowhere to land below; they tire and fall. That is exactly what the Exodus story describes. The episode in which a wind from West Africa swept the locusts that had come to Egypt from the Arabian desert into the sea was not pure imagination. Even today, the same process repeats when locusts strike Egypt.

Why Couldn’t the Locust Swarms Do More Damage in Nepal?

The same thing happened when the last locust swarms arrived in Nepal. Carried by the wind, they reached Nepal’s hills, which acted as a barrier, and the locusts were wiped out.

The locust is a desert insect. It needs warmth. In the cold, its muscles slow down and its flight weakens, and in damp places it contracts fungal disease. The swarms that entered Nepal in 2020 didn’t settle in the Tarai; they headed straight up into the hills. Climbing north from Chitwan, they reached as high as 5,400 meters.

The hills were cold, and the air was very damp. They could neither survive for many days nor lay eggs there. An insect that had flown thousands of kilometers from the Arabian Desert was stopped by our hills.

Any locust swarm is finished within three months, no matter what.

What happens to the surviving locusts after a swarm collapses?

Finally, the most intriguing question: what happens to the locusts that survive after a swarm collapses?

Most locusts die on the journey, but a collapsing swarm doesn’t mean every locust dies. A few survive, and some even lay eggs.

The young that hatch from eggs laid by survivors of a collapsed swarm emerge as locusts for a time. But once the swarm scatters, the bumping of legs stops. No serotonin signal arrives. The padlock on the DNA begins to close again, and the genes that make a locust fall silent.

A few hours of bumping legs is enough to turn a grasshopper into a locust. Turning a locust back into a grasshopper takes generations.

As the crowd thins, the young born from the eggs grow increasingly solitary with each generation, and their young even more so. In this way, after two or three generations, the green color returns, the wings grow shorter, and their nature turns back toward hiding.

After a few generations, they become grasshoppers again. Some scatter back across the same desert, turn green, and start living alone. When there is no swarm, the locust is right there in the desert: few in number, scattered, unnoticed by anyone. This is called the recession period.

In the End, We Are the Locusts’ Story

The final truth is this: an hour is enough to slip unknowingly into a locust crowd. It takes three generations to return to your old form after a locust crowd. And a locust swarm is as unstable as it is terrifying: it is wiped out within three months.

This unstable and destructive journey of the locust may be a mirror of our own society. Last year, stirred by a storm of anger, pain, and conspiracy, did we turn from human beings into locusts? Who knows what invisible ‘serotonin’ whipped up the psychology of our crowd, but we have forgotten the very core of our civilization: ‘Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam,’ the world is one family.

Reading the petty comments on social media, you can see we are busy demonizing one another, exactly like the locusts’ all-devouring march. Social decency and morality are gone; we have forgotten how to be human.

The people who left vulgar, abusive comments and jeers on a video posted by a woman pleading for help in finding her husband, who was lost in a flood, belong to our own society. Just as those who, last year, burned the ward offices, hospitals, and schools of their own villages in revenge were also part of our society.

A locust swarm is wiped out within a few weeks or months. After a few generations, they may even revert to grasshoppers. But as for us, lost like locusts on a journey of cruelty, I have found no grounds to hope that we can become human again.

At a glance: how a grasshopper becomes a locust, and how it turns back.

Grasshoppers and locusts: frequently asked questions

Are locusts and grasshoppers the same?

Every locust is a grasshopper, but not every grasshopper is a locust. Of about 7,000 grasshopper species, only around a dozen can become locusts when crowded.

How does a grasshopper turn into a locust?

When grasshoppers are forced together, their hind legs keep bumping and serotonin rises. Serotonin switches on a protein called PKA, which changes their behavior and, through methyl and acetyl tags on the DNA, switches grasshopper genes off and locust genes on.

How long does a locust swarm last?

A locust lives barely three months. To survive, a swarm must keep laying eggs, and without rain it can’t, so the swarm collapses on its own.

When did locust swarms reach Nepal?

Swarms that came through Chitwan in 1996 caused heavy crop losses. During the 2020 COVID pandemic, swarms reached 55 districts but were stopped by the cold hills.

Can a locust turn back into a grasshopper?

Yes. Once the crowd thins, the bumping stops, serotonin falls, and over two or three generations they become green, solitary grasshoppers again.
