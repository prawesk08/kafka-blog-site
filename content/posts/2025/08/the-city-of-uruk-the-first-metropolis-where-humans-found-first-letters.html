The First Metropolis Where Humans Found Their First Letters

Uruk rose from the clay plains of southern Mesopotamia to become humanity's first great metropolis.

“He who saw the Deep, the foundation of the land,
who knew the ways, was wise in all things.”

So begins The Epic of Gilgamesh—a tale of kingship, grief, immortality, and a flood that once swallowed the world. Composed around 2800 BCE, it is the oldest surviving piece of literature in human history. Its central figure is Gilgamesh, a king of Uruk who had risen to semi-divine stature even before the poem was set down. The city at the heart of the epic, Uruk, would go on to become the cultural and political center of Sumerian civilization in the fourth millennium BCE.

In short

  • The first true metropolis: By the mid-fourth millennium BCE, Uruk surpassed Eridu to become a city of over 50,000 people, powered by secular kingship, mass-produced pottery, and river trade.
  • The birth of writing: Around 3200 BCE, clay and cut reeds came together in Uruk to create cuneiform—transforming accounting lists of barley and beer into humanity's first permanent memory.
  • Everyday Sumer: Behind Uruk's kiln-fired walls and glowing White Temple lay smoky alleys, thirty varieties of beer, drinking songs, sharp inequality, and the earliest industrial workshops.

The Rise of the First Metropolis

As sea levels stabilized around 5000 BCE, the southern coastlines of Mesopotamia began to change. What had once been coastal marshes gradually turned into salt flats. Meanwhile, farther north, Uruk—strategically located near the convergence of rivers and canals—began to thrive. Long before it became the setting of the world's first great epic, Uruk was quietly replacing Eridu as the spiritual and economic center of Sumer. The verses of Gilgamesh would come later, in an age when kings were mythologized and floods became metaphors.

In the fourth millennium BCE, power began to shift across the city-states. Where priests had once governed city life, clan leaders and emerging kings now ruled. The era that followed—known as the Uruk period, named after the city itself—saw the rise of city-states led by secular rulers. By the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, Uruk had grown into one of the largest and most influential cities in Mesopotamia, with a population of over 50,000—larger than any city the world had yet seen.

Archaeologists now link the drastic stylistic shift in pottery during this time with the cultural handover from Eridu to Uruk. Pottery from the earlier Ubaid and Eridu periods was refined and handsome, crafted on the slow wheel and painted with intricate geometric patterns. Those vessels were expensive and used primarily by elites.

In Uruk, potters developed a new technique: the fast wheel. It enabled mass production to meet the demands of a rapidly growing population—though at the expense of craftsmanship. Yet even as pottery lost its elegance, daily life improved in other ways. For the first time in human history, people were no longer limited to eating only what they produced themselves. Boats navigated the rivers and canals, carrying food and goods from neighboring regions. During this period, Sumerians also began venturing far beyond their homeland—into Babylonia, Elam, and even the Anatolian and European highlands.

All of this took place more than five thousand years ago. By then, Sumerian civilization was already two thousand years old—a span of time as long as the centuries separating us from Julius Caesar. Yet the first Egyptian pyramids would not rise for another nine centuries, and the first stones of Stonehenge would not be set in place for another thirteen hundred years.

The First Light of History: Writing Is Born (3200 BCE)

When gas clouds compacted by their own weight build enough pressure and gravity, the first stars burst into being. When enough people gather in one place, a settlement develops a gravity of its own—drawing still more people toward it. As the size of a settlement increases, so does the pressure on its systems. By the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, as Uruk expanded into a true metropolis, its population density—and the sheer complexity of its daily interactions—gave rise to an even more intricate form of human organization.

The first stars of human settlement burst into light in Uruk sometime around 3200 BCE. A later Sumerian epic, Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta, attributes the invention of writing to the “Lord of Kullaba,” a king overwhelmed by too many messages for his courier to hold in memory:

“Because the messenger’s mouth was heavy and he couldn’t repeat the message,
the Lord of Kullaba patted some clay and put words on it like a tablet.
Until then, there had been no putting words on clay.”

The Sumerians had two things in abundance: clay beneath their feet, and reeds growing thick along the riverbanks. Together, they formed humanity's first writing system. Scribes would pat a lump of wet clay into roughly the size of a modern smartphone, cut a reed into a wedge, and press it into the surface to leave crisp impressions. Those distinctive wedge shapes gave the script its name: cuneiform.

Pressed into damp river clay with a cut reed, early cuneiform turned everyday ration tallies into humanity's first written records.

The earliest cuneiform signs were pictographs—simple images depicting everyday objects so that anyone could read them at a glance. A fish meant fish. A scratched circle stood for a head of cattle. A jar symbolized oil. Initially, these signs were used to keep track of supplies, taxes, and rations. This pictographic system, known as proto-cuneiform, emerged around 3300 BCE. Its first records may have been dry—lists of goats, measures of barley, allocations of beer—but their implications were monumental.

The oldest excavated cuneiform tablet dates to around 3200 BCE. It depicts a bowl of food with an eating mouth beside six impressions, and a sheaf of wheat beside five—indicating that a worker could exchange the tablet for six bowls of food or five sheaves of wheat. Scribes would have had to work swiftly, turning out hundreds of such documents every day.

Over time, these pictographs grew simpler and more abstract as scribes invented shortcuts and standardized their strokes. Before long, the signs no longer resembled the objects they represented. By the start of the third millennium BCE, the inventory of signs had shrunk from more than 1,500 to around 600. Around the same time, someone had a brilliant idea: each symbol could stand for a specific sound rather than an entire object or concept. That leap laid the foundation for phonetic writing—the earliest step toward an alphabet.

With this innovation, however, came exclusivity. Only an educated class of scribes could decipher the new signs, and a specialized profession was born. The human mind would never be the same. For the first time, knowledge could outlive speech and memory.

As the clay dried, those letters remained etched into history. That is why we can still play Sumerian music and read Sumerian epics today. People could hear and read the words of kings and scribes who had died centuries before. Authority could now be transferred across distance and generations, not merely spoken aloud. Just as importantly, people could record everything they had learned so that it would not only be remembered, but built upon. Partly because of this newfound capacity to accumulate knowledge, Sumerian technology would take even greater leaps in the next phase of its history.

Walls, Temples, and the Rhythm of Daily Life

By 3000 BCE, Uruk stood as the epicenter of Sumerian administration, agriculture, and ritual. Its scribes had etched thousands of tablets. Its merchants ferried goods across rivers and marshes. Its temples functioned simultaneously as banks, warehouses, and courts of law.

It is in this city that The Epic of Gilgamesh opens—the world's oldest literary work. Its hero, two-thirds god and one-third man, may well have been a real king who ruled Uruk around 2700 BCE.

Though the epic is more myth than chronicle, it reveals how deeply life in Uruk was changing in the early third millennium BCE. For one, warfare was beginning to intensify across the region, and city-states now needed walls. In the poem, Uruk's fortifications are celebrated with fierce pride, hailed again and again as “Strong-Walled Uruk.” The narrator calls out:

“Go up on the wall of Uruk and walk around,
examine its foundation, inspect its brickwork thoroughly.
Is not even the core of the structure made of kiln-fired brick?
Did not the Seven Sages themselves lay out its plan?”

— The Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet I

The epic also gives us a glimpse of how the city was partitioned, hinting at deliberate urban planning by its rulers:

“These parts comprise Uruk:
one third for the city,
one third for garden,
one third for field and the precinct for the temple of Ishtar.”

Uruk had a bustling port on the river, lined with workshops and tightly packed houses. Rising at the center of it all was Uruk's famed White Temple of Ishtar—elevated twenty-one meters above the plain and coated in white plaster so that it gleamed brilliantly in the desert sun. At the height of the Uruk period, the city spanned 2.5 square kilometers and housed more than 50,000 people.

At the heart of Uruk's maze of mudbrick alleys rose its elevated temple precinct, visible for miles across the plain.

In the wealthier quarters of town, homes were constructed from baked brick; elsewhere, they were built of sun-dried clay. Affluent neighborhoods featured houses with intricate arches and domes. Inside, residents laid down layers of clay, crushed gypsum dust, and reed matting to create a smooth, carpet-like floor. At night, families routinely slept on their rooftops, escaping the stifling heat trapped inside the mudbrick rooms.

The city itself would have assaulted the senses with a pungent mixture of smells. Pottery kilns and brickworks belched smoke throughout the day. There was no drainage system, and residents tossed their household refuse directly into the streets. Houses clustered together chaotically, forming a labyrinth of narrow alleys draped overhead with reed matting to shade passersby from the blistering midday sun.

Beer, Laments, and the Dawn of Mass Production

Walk down the streets of Uruk in that era and you would pass stalls piled high with lentils and beans, pomegranates and dates, and clay jars brimming with syrup and oil. Farmers trudged by carrying heavy bundles of reeds and wheat on their backs. Herders drove long-haired sheep and oxen through the gates. In shaded courtyards, men gathered in circles around a single large jar of beer set in the center, sipping it together through straws made from hollow reeds. Although the Sumerians knew how to make wine, it was beer they loved above all else.

Brewed directly from wheat and millet, Sumerian beer came in more than thirty distinct varieties—pale, dark, cloudy, infused with herbs, or sweetened with honey. The cheaper brews still had bits of grain floating on the surface. One cuneiform tablet from the early third millennium BCE preserves one of their drinking songs:

“I will summon brewers and cupbearers to serve us floods of beer and pass it around.
What pleasure! What delight!
Blissfully to take it in.
To sing jubilantly of this noble liquor.
Our hearts are enchanted and our souls radiant.”

Just as tablets recorded details of Sumerian musical instruments, they also captured the everyday conversations of ordinary people. From those clay records, we can eavesdrop on what citizens talked about in their courtyards and taverns over shared jars of beer—and their worries feel unmistakably familiar.

On one tablet, a frustrated man laments: “I am a thoroughbred steed, but I am hitched to a mule.” Life for the poor was harsh, often forcing families to borrow food and silver from predatory lenders. Another cuneiform tablet captures their quiet despair: “The poor man is better dead than alive; if he has bread, he has no salt; if he has salt, he has no water.”

Because coined money did not yet exist, workers were paid directly in food and essential goods. The minimum ration for an unskilled laborer was twenty liters of barley per month, supplemented by two liters of oil and two kilos of wool per year. Supervisors earned twice as much.

This era also marked the dawn of mass production. Sumerian clay tablets paint a vivid portrait of a booming industrial economy. In the city of Girsu, for example, fifteen thousand women were employed in the textile industry. One large workshop produced eleven hundred tons of flour a year, along with bread, beer, and linseed oil—employing 134 specialists and 858 skilled workers, the vast majority of them women.

From bustling workshops and shared jars of beer to maritime ambition, Uruk's urban experiment reshaped the ancient world.

Beyond Uruk: The Road to Ur and the Bronze Age

Uruk's transformation sent ripples far across the ancient world, inspiring rival cities to rise around it. As the third millennium BCE unfolded, one of those Sumerian neighbors—Ur—gradually surpassed Uruk in power and prestige. Positioned where river met sea, Ur would emerge as a hub of maritime trade and a beacon of art and architecture. The world was stepping into the Bronze Age, and Sumer's legacy was about to sail farther than its rivers had ever flowed.
