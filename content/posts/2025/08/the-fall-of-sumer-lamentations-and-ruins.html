Lamentations and Ruins: How Drought, Invasion, and Shifting Rivers Ended the World’s First Civilization

Sumer’s cities, once blazing with temples and the first lines of writing, faded under the weight of drought, invasion, and time. In their ruins linger the echoes of a people who gave us civilization’s dawn, only to vanish into its earliest dusk.

The ancient Sumerians who witnessed the destruction of their cities answered their sorrow with poetry, as humans have always done. For each of their great ruined cities, they composed a lament. One of these poems, The Lament for Ur, carries across four millennia the raw, tangible anguish of that catastrophe:

“The gods have abandoned us. Like migrating birds, they have gone. Ur is destroyed. Bitter is its lament. The country’s blood now fills its holes like hot bronze in a mold. Bodies dissolve like fat in the sun. Our temple is destroyed. Smoke lies on our city like a shroud. Blood flows as the river does, the lamenting of men and women. Sadness abounds. Ur is no more.”

When Ur fell around 2000 BCE, Sumerian scribes turned grief into enduring poetry—preserving the sound of a collapsing world in clay.

The fall of Ur was one of the great turning points in ancient history. Sacked and burned by the Elamites sometime around 2000 BCE, the imperial capital never recovered its supremacy. Over the decades that followed, rival city-states fought over the ruins of the former empire, and the wars of that era reduced Sumerian cities to blackened shells of burnt clay. The Lament for Ur adds:

“The people mourn. Its people like potsherds littering the approaches. The walls were gaping. The high gates, the roads, were piled with dead. In all the streets and roadways, bodies lay. In open fields, that used to fill with dancers, the people lay in heaps.”

This marked the final death of Sumerian society. The first human civilization—one the peoples of Sumer and Akkad had built over an astonishing three thousand five hundred years—was no longer in existence.

In short

After Utu-Hengal and Ur-Nammu sparked the brief Neo-Sumerian Renaissance (the Third Dynasty of Ur), Ur-Nammu’s death in a Gutian ambush in 2094 BCE set off a century-long unraveling. Salinized soil, relentless drought, and Martu incursions overwhelmed even King Shu-Sin’s 300-kilometer Martu Wall. Under the last Sumerian king, Ibbi-Sin, rebellion, betrayal by his general Ishbi-Erra, and an Elamite siege ended Ur around 2000 BCE—leaving Sumerian a sacred temple language while shifting rivers buried Ur and Uruk beneath the desert sands.

The Twilight of the Third Dynasty: Ur-Nammu’s Last Campaign

Utu-Hengal had defeated the last Gutian king, Tirigan, and returned power to the people of Sumer. He laid the foundation for a Neo-Sumerian empire remembered as the Third Dynasty of Ur—or, more evocatively, the Sumerian Renaissance. Utu-Hengal’s successor, Ur-Nammu, despite his mysterious rise to power, turned out to be a brilliant administrator and builder, raising great ziggurats and overseeing the legal and civic reforms that defined this final flowering.

The Guti had retreated to the mountains and resumed their nomadic ways, yet they remained a persistent threat to Sumerian towns—raiding cities and stealing cattle just as they always had. Putting an end to those raids became the driving focus of King Ur-Nammu’s reign. He raised an army and marched into Gutium, determined to break the Gutian threat once and for all. Instead, he was killed in one of the classic Gutian ambushes in the mountain passes.

The death of this last great builder-king triggered the final downward spiral of Sumerian culture. The loss of so beloved a ruler was commemorated in a lengthy epic known as The Death of Ur-Nammu:

“He who was beloved by the troops could not raise his neck anymore. The wise one lay down. Silence descended, as he, who was the vigor of the land, had fallen. The land becomes demolished like a mountain. Like a cypress forest, it was stripped, its appearance changed.”

Ur-Nammu’s death in battle in 2094 BCE shocked the empire and inspired one of Sumer’s most poignant royal elegies.

The poem follows Ur-Nammu as he descends into the underworld and presents his offerings to the gods who dwell there. From the afterlife, the epic places a final, heartbroken lament on the king’s lips:

“Now just as the rain pouring down from heaven cannot turn back, I can never return to see the beautiful bricks of Ur.”

Drought, Famine, and the Martu Wall

Ur-Nammu was killed in the Gutian ambush in 2094 BCE, and four Sumerian kings would follow him on the throne. His immediate successor, King Shulgi, enjoyed real success on the battlefield and reformed the economy as thoroughly as he could. Yet the reigns of the kings who followed him were haunted by unrelenting pressure from the wild mountain regions.

The drought was still biting hard, and the irrigated farmland was growing steadily choked by accumulating layers of salt. As harvests shrank and food grew scarce, more and more nomadic groups were driven to raiding and plundering simply to survive. By now, the Guti were not the only nomadic tribe who threatened the borders of Sumer.

Driven southward by drought and hunger, the Semitic-speaking Martu emerged as the most formidable nomadic threat on Sumer’s western frontier.

One group in particular, known as the Martu, posed the gravest danger. The Martu were Semitic-speaking, sheep-herding nomads from the highlands of Syria and Lebanon. Much as they had viewed the Guti, the Sumerians regarded the Martu as wild and barbarous, describing them in terms steeped in contempt and dread:

“The hostile Martu have entered the plains. The Martu, a ravaging people with canine instinct, like wolves.”

“The Martu, the powerful south wind, who from the remote past have not known cities. The Martu, who know no grain. The Martu, who know no house or town. The savages of the mountains, the Martu who eat raw meat, who are not buried after their death.”

It is clear that by this time, the Martu were finding their nomadic way of life in the Syrian highlands increasingly unsustainable. Relentless drought and famine were pushing them south—out of the arid hills and into the rich farmland of the river valley, straight into Sumer. Despite its waning strength, the Sumerian state remained determined to hold back the Martu incursions. The new king, Shu-Sin, who came after Shulgi’s line, even ordered the construction of a colossal wall stretching between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers.

Building such a barrier was an extraordinary feat of engineering, proving that even in its twilight years, the Sumerian state could still mobilize immense manpower and organizational energy. Known as the Martu Wall, it ran roughly three hundred kilometers long—more than twice the length of Hadrian’s Wall in Britain. Archaeologists have never been able to locate its physical remains, so we can only guess what form it took. Yet its strategic vision is unmistakable: the Martu Wall aimed to turn the fertile plains between the Tigris and the Euphrates into an island fortress—a river on either flank, the sea to the south, and a great fortified wall to the north.

Still, like every wall in history, the Martu Wall was only as strong as the garrison constantly manning it. As the weakened Sumerian state lost the capacity to hold the line, the barrier was bound to fail. In the end, raising that wall was not an act of imperial confidence, but the desperate last resort of an empire caving in on itself. Walls work only until the invaders build bigger ladders.

Ibbi-Sin and the Sack of Ur

The last Sumerian king was a man named Ibbi-Sin, who took the throne in 2028 BCE. Almost as soon as he was crowned, the empire began to fracture. In the very first year of his reign, the eastern city of Eshnunna broke free from imperial control.

Early in Ibbi-Sin’s reign, vassal cities from Eshnunna to Susa broke away, leaving Ur isolated against mounting external pressure.

An even larger rebellion erupted in the third year of his reign, when the city of Susa, in the region of Elam, successfully threw off Ur’s rule. These Elamites—people of the Iranian lowlands—had lived under Sumerian domination and were now beginning to flex their own military muscle. Their resurgence would soon sound the death knell of the Sumerian Age.

The unraveling empire could no longer sustain its defenses along the Martu Wall. In the fifth year of Ibbi-Sin’s reign, the great barrier built by his predecessors gave way. The Martu poured across the frontier and overran the rich farmlands behind it. The consequences were immediate and catastrophic. Food shortages swept the realm; by the seventh and eighth years of his reign, grain prices had surged as much as sixty-fold. Famine struck the capital hardest of all, and people starved to death in the streets of Ur.

Caught between invading armies and spreading starvation, Ibbi-Sin spiraled into panic. One remarkable letter written by his general, Ishbi-Erra, still survives today. Sent to buy grain from the city of Isin, Ishbi-Erra wrote back to his king with a stark, firsthand glimpse of a society coming apart at the seams:

“You ordered me to travel to Isin and Kazallu to purchase grain. With grain reaching the exchange rate of one shekel of silver per gur, 20 talents of silver have been invested in the purchase. But I have heard news that the hostile Martu have entered inside your territories. I entered with the entire amount of grain, and now I have let the Martu, all of them, penetrate inside the land. Because of the Martu, I am unable to hand over this grain for threshing. They are stronger than me, while I am condemned to sitting around.”

The Akkadian general Ishbi-Erra had no intention of returning to the starving capital. Soon he abandoned King Ibbi-Sin altogether, carving out his own realm and declaring himself king of Isin and Nippur. Besieged from every direction, Ibbi-Sin threw himself into a frantic series of last-ditch measures. He reinforced the fortifications of Ur and Nippur, but none of it could stop the tide. Piece by piece, city by city, the Sumerian Empire dissolved until only Ur itself remained standing, ringed by hostile forces.

Before long, the people of Elam—former subjects of Sumer from the foothills of Iran—marched down along the mountain paths with a coalition of tribesmen and laid siege to the great city of Ur. Ibbi-Sin made one final, desperate gamble to repel them, appealing even to the traitor Ishbi-Erra and the hostile Martu for help, but it came to nothing. The Elamites breached the walls of Ur and put the city to the torch. They stormed into the sacred precincts and the ziggurat, slaughtering priests and plundering treasures as they went.

Elamite armies breached Ur’s defenses around 2000 BCE, burning its temples, poisoning its canals, and carrying the last Sumerian king away in chains.

The surrounding fields were torched, and the waterways became fouled with disease. Elamite soldiers broke into the royal palace and seized Ibbi-Sin. They bound him in chains, marched him back across the border to their homeland, and locked him away. He was the last king of Sumer—a civilization that had endured for more than three thousand years. Ibbi-Sin would die in chains, a prisoner of his enemies in a foreign land.

A Post-Sumerian World and a Sacred Dead Language

The fall of Ur stands as one of history’s defining watersheds. It ended the era of a unified Sumerian state and plunged southern Mesopotamia into a fragmented age in which rival city-states scrambled to control the ruins of the old empire. Meanwhile, the drought dragged on, and the salt-choked fields refused to yield barley. Facing hunger and ruin, the Sumerian people began fleeing their homeland in huge numbers, carrying what meager belongings they could—along with their laments for the great fallen cities.

Over the centuries that followed, a vast migration unfolded from southern Mesopotamia toward the north. Many Sumerian speakers settled in Akkadian lands, soon joined by the Martu, who were themselves settling down inside the cities they had overrun. Fleeing Sumerians, migrating Martu, and native Akkadians mingled together, blending their traditions just as the peoples of this river valley had always done. From that cultural fusion rose the next great chapter of Mesopotamian history—and the region’s new superpowers, the Babylonian and Assyrian empires.

Sumerian itself was now a dead language. It would never again be heard in bustling streets or noisy markets, and gradually it faded from everyday memory. Yet it survived inside the temples, much as medieval European churches preserved Latin long after the fall of Rome. Later Mesopotamian rulers kept Sumerian alive in their shrines and scriptures for at least another two thousand years. To these later generations, Sumerian became the language of the gods, and the ancient kings of Ur and Uruk passed into legend—some even revered as gods themselves.

For the next two millennia, every great king of Mesopotamia, whether in Babylon or Assyria, claimed legitimacy under a single resonant title—King of Sumer and Akkad—reaching back to the first age, the dawn of humanity’s journey into civilization.

When the Rivers Left: The Slow Death of Ur

Even after the collapse of the Sumerian Empire, Ur did not vanish overnight. It endured as a population center, rising and falling several times over the centuries of the post-Sumerian era.

In the end, however, it was the landscape that had given birth to Ur, and it was the landscape that sealed its fate. Today, the Persian Gulf that once nourished Ur’s harbors lies nowhere near its ruins. When archaeologists unearthed Ur in 1815 CE, deep in the desert, they were astonished to find millions of seashells scattered across the site. Over the millennia, the steady buildup of river silt—combined with shifting global sea levels—pushed Iraq’s gulf coastline far back to its present edge, roughly one hundred kilometers south of Ur.

Once a thriving port near the Persian Gulf and the Euphrates, Ur now sits surrounded by arid desert—about a hundred kilometers from the modern coast.

The Euphrates River, which had once carried the bounties of northern trade straight to Ur’s quays, also abandoned the city, shifting its channel over the centuries. Today, nothing surrounds the ruins except miles of arid desert. Water had always been Ur’s lifeblood; losing both the sea and the river condemned the city to a slow, quiet death.

Gradually, people left its houses and lanes behind. They stopped tending the fields and clearing the irrigation canals; the earth cracked dry, and the fertile topsoil blew away on the wind. Priests extinguished the sacred fire in the upper chamber of the ziggurat and ceased bringing offerings to the moon god, Sin. The markets shut down, mudbrick walls crumbled, and timber roof beams rotted and collapsed.

Desert winds and drifting sand rolled through the empty streets, and dunes buried the fallen ramparts. Before long, the greatest city the early world had ever known was reduced to a lonely mound of earth, passed only by the occasional desert traveler. It was among these very ruins that the Italian traveler Pietro Della Valle once took shelter with his wife while hiding from bandits—and through him that Europe first glimpsed the scattered fragments of writing the Sumerians had left behind in their forgotten script. Somewhere beneath the silent mounds of Ur lay the clay tablets bearing The Lament for Ur:

“May that storm swoop down no more on your city. May the door be closed on it like the great city gate at nighttime. Until distant days, other days, future days. In your city reduced to ruin mounds may a lament be made to you. Oh Nanna, may your restored city be resplendent before you.”

Desert Mounds and the Voices That Survived in Clay

Following the sack of Ur around 2000 BCE, the ancient metropolis of Uruk also fell into steep decline, and much of its population fled. Uruk did enjoy a later revival when it served as a regional capital under the Assyrian Empire. Yet when the Euphrates shifted its course there too, Uruk was abandoned completely.

The eroded walls of Uruk—immortalized in The Epic of Gilgamesh—still rise fifteen meters above the desert floor, littered with ancient potsherds.

Even today, the walls of Uruk—the very walls celebrated so vividly in The Epic of Gilgamesh—remain visible. Long ridges of ancient brickwork trace a flat, lunar desert landscape, still rising fifteen meters high around a city washed by a quiet tide of broken pottery and bleached bones. The English archaeologist William Loftus was the first European to reach Uruk. When he came upon its ruins in 1849 CE, he was struck by the haunting sight of those immense mounds rising from the wilderness, later writing: “Of all the desolate sights I ever beheld, that of Uruk incomparably surpasses all.”

Across all the ancient cities of Sumer—in Nippur and Eridu, in Kish and Lagash, in Ur and Uruk—decay was gradual, yet unstoppable. Windborne sand banked up against standing walls and swallowed the streets; rain and gusts wore down every remaining edifice. The wandering travelers who stumbled across those solitary ruins must have traded quiet stories about what could have happened to the people who built such staggering monuments and then vanished from the earth.

With their cities in ruins, the Sumerians slipped out of living history. Even the empires that succeeded them—the ones that kept Sumerian alive in their temples and retold tales of its kings—eventually crumbled into ruin themselves. The knowledge of how to read Sumerian was lost, and its history turned to dust. Only the clay tablets endured, sleeping beneath the sands of southern Iraq—fragile vessels holding the voices of an entire people, waiting for archaeologists to unearth them and, through arduous, painstaking scholarship, learn to read them once again.

Buried beneath desert sand for millennia, Sumerian clay tablets preserve everything from grain ledgers and recipes to timeless meditations on mortality.

Those fragments send us sudden bursts of light from the most distant human past. They preserve the Sumerians’ ledgers and recipes, their music and prayers, their loves and griefs, their triumphs and their heartbreaking laments for the world’s first cities. And they preserve the wistful philosophy of those earliest writers, captured in lines from The Epic of Gilgamesh:

“Nobody sees death. Nobody sees the face of death. Nobody hears the voice of death. Savage death just cuts mankind down. Sometimes we build a house, sometimes we make a nest, but then brothers divide it upon inheritance. Sometimes, there is hostility in the land, but the river rises and brings floodwater. Dragonflies drift on the river, their faces look upon the face of the sun, but then suddenly there is nothing.”

Outlived by the Woolly Mammoths

Before I bring this series to a close, I want to return one last time to the woolly mammoths. When the Sumerians first reached Iraq’s southern coast around 5500 BCE, the very last woolly mammoths on earth were fighting for survival on the opposite side of the planet.

They had made Wrangel Island their final refuge and clung to existence there, even as the Sumerians were sowing the seeds of humanity’s first civilization in southern Iraq. On that small, rocky island on the rim of the Arctic Ocean, the last woolly mammoths finally lay down and died around 1700 BCE—at a time when the last kings of Ur were already a distant memory.

Sumerian society, in its imperial form, had risen, lived out its golden age for more than three thousand years, and passed into silence—outlived by the woolly mammoth.
