A New Age of Trade and Warfare

In the year 1614 CE, an Italian nobleman named Pietro Della Valle left Venice and sailed to Constantinople. His travels carried him through Turkey, Egypt, and Syria before he reached Iraq in October 1616. In Baghdad, he fell in love with Sitti Maani, an Assyrian Christian noblewoman, and married her within a few months.

In early 1617, while traveling with his new wife and local guides, the party spotted local tribesmen on the distant horizon. Fearing trouble, their guides searched for a place to take cover. In the distance, they made out a sprawling cluster of enormous ruins. While the guides negotiated safe passage with a nearby tribal leader, Della Valle and his companions sheltered among the ruins for several days.

In short

  • Ur's maritime golden age: Positioned where the Euphrates met the sea, Ur forged bronze from imported copper and tin, traded with the Indus Valley, and amassed legendary wealth captured on the Standard of Ur.
  • The birth of organized warfare: Around 2500 BCE, the slaving city of Lagash shattered millennia of relative peace, memorializing its slaughter of Umma on the brutal Stele of the Vultures.
  • Revenge and the Akkadian dawn: Umma's rebel king Lugal-zage-si burned Lagash and claimed the land “between the seas,” only to overstretch his realm and clear the path for Sargon of Akkad.

Pietro Della Valle: First Encounter with Sumerian Ruins

During the day, Della Valle passed the hours wandering among those monumental remains. He would later write:

“Our removal hence being still deferred, I went in the forenoon to take a more diligent view of the ruins of above said ancient building. What it had been, I could not understand. But I have found it have been built with very good bricks, most of which were stamped with certain unknown letters, which appeared very ancient.”

Della Valle was transfixed by the broken fragments of writing scattered across the ground, stamped again and again into stone and clay brick alike. Of these curious marks, he wrote further:

“Amongst other symbols which I discovered in that short time, two I find in many places, one resembled a pyramid while other had a head with seven stars. It looked like they were some kind of seal.”

Bricks stamped with mysterious wedge-shaped letters at Ur gave seventeenth-century Europe its first glimpse of Sumerian writing.

A few days later, once the guides had secured safe passage, Della Valle's party moved on. When he left, he carried several of those cuneiform tablets with him. For the first time, Europeans were being directly introduced to the ancient writing system forged by the Sumerians in the fourth millennium BCE.

The City of Ur: The Port That Forged Bronze

At the time, Della Valle could not have known that the ruins he had explored were those of the ancient Sumerian city of Ur—its identification would not be confirmed until 1815. By the middle of the third millennium BCE, Ur had replaced Uruk as the beating heart of Sumerian civilization.

In the fourth millennium BCE, the epicenter of Sumer had been the great walled city of Uruk. Sitting at the convergence of rivers and canal networks, Uruk was the world's first true metropolis. Its formidable walls and surging population made it the region's de facto leader. Yet Uruk's rise sent ripples far beyond its borders, and as the fourth millennium drew to a close, rival cities of comparable grandeur began to rise around it.

Where the Euphrates once met the Persian Gulf, Ur grew into Mesopotamia's premier maritime gateway.

Among them, Ur gradually supplanted Uruk as the center of Mesopotamia in the opening centuries of the third millennium BCE. It would flourish into realms of untold wealth, pushing the boundaries of what humanity thought possible.

Ur stood right where the Euphrates River met the sea. It was a bustling trading port and fishing town where seagulls wheeled overhead and fishermen bartered their catches—fish, oysters, and turtles—along its docks. Straddling both river and coast made Ur the booming hub of regional commerce. If you needed clay or reeds, southern Iraq had them in abundance; for virtually everything else, the Sumerians had to trade.

Blessed with fertile silt and sophisticated irrigation, the Sumerians harvested rich surpluses year after year. Alone among the peoples of the ancient Middle East, they produced both abundance and extraordinary variety. Archaeologists argue that ancient Mesopotamia enjoyed a far richer and more diverse diet than not only its contemporaries, but even some later Bronze Age civilizations. That culinary richness survives on clay tablets, including ancient Sumerian recipes. One recipe for a stew called Tuh’u captures the flavors they savored:

“Get the water ready, add fat, salt, beer, onion, rocket, coriander, semolina, cumin and beetroot. Add them to the cooking pot, then pound leek and garlic together and add water. Let all blend and reduce to a pulp, then sprinkle with coriander and carrot.”

Boats from Ur: A New Age of Global Trade

Boats laden with wheat and grain, dried reeds, and figs now sailed upriver, carrying food to neighboring lands. In return, prized resources flowed back toward Sumer.

For ordinary construction, timber could be felled in the Zagros Mountains. For finer work—palaces and ornate city gates—only the fragrant wood of the cedar tree would do. Cedar was shipped from the high mountain passes of Lebanon. In The Epic of Gilgamesh, one famous episode even recounts the “king’s quest to slay a monster in the mountains of Lebanon and steal the sacred trees it guarded.”

Silver floated down the Euphrates on barges from Turkey's Taurus Mountains, while gold arrived overland from Egypt and by sea from India. Copper came down from the mountains of northwestern Iran and later by ship from the island of Cyprus. Tin traveled along the rugged mountain passes of Afghanistan, as it would throughout the Bronze Age.

Here in Ur, copper and tin were first alloyed into bronze—a technology that would define the next epoch of human history. In the centuries that followed, Ur remained central to the Bronze Age world as the primary port through which tin flowed.

The Sumerians traded on a scale we would recognize today as truly globalized. From their narrow coast on the Persian Gulf, ships sailed out to ports in modern Bahrain and Oman. From there, they hugged the shoreline eastward to trade with another enigmatic civilization—the people of the Indus Valley in northern India. At the height of Ur's power, the Indus Valley Civilization was entering its mature Harappan phase.

From those distant trading partners of the Indus Valley, Sumerians acquired spices and gemstones of every kind. The brilliant blue lapis lazuli they adored was quarried and routed through the trade networks linked to the Indus Valley cities. Sumerian artisans fashioned it into jewelry, amulets, gaming-board inlays, musical instruments, and sculptures of astonishing beauty.

All of that commerce funneled through Ur, swelling the city to a level of wealth no human settlement had ever achieved before. Grave goods unearthed from the tombs of Ur's kings and ordinary citizens alike reveal not only the staggering riches of its rulers, but also a level of craftsmanship that speaks to a thriving, sophisticated community of artists.

One artifact recovered from a royal tomb in Ur offers an extraordinary window into the lives and manners of the ancient Sumerians: an ornate, box-like piece of decorative furniture inlaid with a mosaic of shell, red limestone, and lapis lazuli.

The Standard of Ur captures Sumerian society in vivid mosaic—chariots and spears on its War panel, farmers, feasts, and scribes on its Peace panel.

Today we call it “The Standard of Ur.” Its panels depict intricate scenes from everyday life. On one side, Sumerians march to war: chariots pulled by donkeys, soldiers in reed skirts and leather helmets, men gripping long spears and battle-axes. On the other side, Sumerians gather in peace: farmers and herders laboring on one register, and above them shaved-headed scribes seated at their desks.

Urbanism in the Sumerian world was reaching its zenith. By the late third millennium BCE, the majority of Sumer's population lived in cities. In this newly urbanized landscape, Ur's economic might remained unmatched. Over the ensuing centuries its political reach expanded and contracted; at one point, Ur's rulers even styled themselves “King of Ur and King of Kish,” claiming authority over a major rival city to the north.

Lagash: The Rise of Warfare and Terror

For more than three thousand years, Sumer had thrived through farming, trade, and shared belief, largely spared from large-scale warfare. By the mid-third millennium BCE, however, Ur's influence began to wane. A harsher, more militarized era was dawning—one in which trade and diplomacy were no longer enough. In this new age of violence, a city named Lagash came into its own.

Lagash was a slaving city. It had grown wealthy by raiding hill villages, kidnapping captives, and selling them across the region. Sometime around 2500 BCE, Lagash clashed with its neighbor, the city of Umma, over a disputed stretch of farmland along the river. The quarrel erupted into war, and one carved stone monument from that conflict—the “Stele of the Vultures”—captures the brutal spirit of the age.

Its upper register looks conventional enough: King Eannatum of Lagash rides ahead of his troops in a chariot, a quiver of spears and javelins at his side, an animal skin draped across his torso. Below, however, Eannatum leads a tight phalanx of soldiers armed with short and long spears straight into a scene of merciless carnage.

The fighting was savage; inscriptions claim that more than four thousand men fell in the battle. As the defeated soldiers of Umma tried to flee, the stele shows Lagash's warriors cutting them down and trampling them underfoot. The monument is unflinching in its portrayal of slaughter.

The Stele of the Vultures—named for the birds of prey carrying away the severed heads of Umma's dead—marks a chilling shift toward terror and imperial conquest.

Something in this carving signals a profound shift in the human mindset of the era. A cruelty that revels in the suffering of enemies appears most starkly in the detail that gave the monument its name: vultures wheeling overhead, gripping the severed heads of fallen Umma soldiers in their beaks and picking at their tongues and eyes. It does not merely record a massacre perpetrated by Lagash—it celebrates it with relish.

After three millennia of growth built on irrigation, commerce, and shared ritual, Sumer was crossing an irreversible threshold into an age of conquest. Powered by its military machine, the slaving city of Lagash went on to subjugate much of southern Mesopotamia. Many historians regard it as the first true empire of the ancient world.

Lugal-zage-si: An Empire Built on Revenge

In that distant age, governing even a single city was daunting. For all its battlefield triumphs, the empire of Lagash was dangerously overstretched. King Eannatum held his short-lived dominion together through campaigns of terror—and, unsurprisingly, revolts soon flared across Sumer.

As the hated empire of Lagash fractured and collapsed, a rebel from one of its conquered cities seized his moment. His name was Lugal-zage-si, and he was the ruler of Umma—the very city whose defeat and humiliation had been carved with such glee onto the Stele of the Vultures. We do not know every secret of Lugal-zage-si's military success, but there is no doubt what drove him: a burning thirst for vengeance against Lagash.

Rising in rebellion, he swiftly toppled the kings still loyal to Lagash in Kish and Larsa. Next, he marched on the great port of Ur and the mighty walled fortress of Uruk. Both fell, and the rebel king moved his capital to Uruk. Finally, he turned on Lagash itself—and there his fiery vengeance burst out in full fury. The city did not hold out for long. Lugal-zage-si stormed over its walls, sacked the city, and burned it to the ground. Even by the harsh standards of the era, it was a shocking act, mourned in horror across several surviving epics.

Driven by vengeance for Umma's humiliation, Lugal-zage-si stormed Lagash and forged a sweeping—if fleeting—Sumerian realm.

After sacking Lagash, Lugal-zage-si's momentum seemed unstoppable. Pressing north along the course of the two rivers, he soon claimed every territory Lagash had once controlled. At the height of his reign, he even boasted of conquering all the land between the Lower Sea and the Upper Sea—meaning the Persian Gulf and the Mediterranean. One of his inscriptions declares:

“The great god Enlil gave kingship of the land to him. The region from the lower sea through the Tigris and Euphrates to the upper sea. Thirty-two kings gathered against him, but he defeated them and smote their cities, prostrated their lords, and destroyed the whole countrysides as far as the silver mines.”

That boast was almost certainly an exaggeration. Up to this point, the Sumerians had never been able to hold distant colonies or occupy far-off territories for long. More likely, Lugal-zage-si led a bold raiding expedition toward the Mediterranean coast, plundering cities and hauling their treasure back to Uruk.

Still, this was the first time any Sumerian prince had ever made such a claim: lordship over the land between the seas. To the Sumerians, the Upper Sea marked the western edge of the known world. The vision of a single monarch uniting all the lands between the two seas planted a seed that would haunt the imagination of every ambitious king who followed him.

Sargon of Akkad: Twilight of the Sumerian Age

Like King Eannatum before him, Lugal-zage-si fell into the trap of overstretching his resources. His realm was simply too vast to hold together. Before long, civil wars and rebellions erupted across Sumer's cities.

As Sumerian city-states tore one another apart, Sargon of Akkad stepped into the breach to usher in a new imperial era.

Into that chaos stepped the other great people of Mesopotamia, sensing their moment to rule. These were the Akkadians—for three thousand years the junior partners of Mesopotamian civilization, living alongside and under Sumerian culture and kings. Soon, one man would lead them into open rebellion against the Sumerian order.

He would go down in history as “Sargon,” a name meaning “the true king” in Akkadian—and he would usher in the twilight of the Sumerian age. Sargon was also a fierce nationalist: he appointed Akkadian officials to run his administration and made Akkadian the official language of his new empire. As the Bronze Age world grew ever more interconnected, Sumerian speech and custom slowly receded, gradually overtaken by Akkadian scripts and culture.

The two peoples of Mesopotamia had been bilingual since antiquity, yet with each passing generation, fewer and fewer Akkadians needed to learn Sumerian—and eventually, even Sumerians themselves stopped speaking it in daily life. Two peoples who had shared the land between the rivers in quiet coexistence since the dawn of history were beginning to drift apart.

The city of Ur would fall and rise countless times over the centuries that followed. Right at the close of the third millennium BCE, it would even surge back to lead a brilliant Sumerian renaissance after a dark age under the Guti. And though Sumerian eventually vanished from everyday speech, it survived for another two thousand years inside temples and ziggurats, revered as the sacred language of the gods.

In the end, it was nature itself—the very force that had birthed this great city—that sealed its final downfall.

The proud port Ur once guarded has vanished without a trace. When archaeologists finally identified the city in 1815 CE, the harbor that had poured boundless wealth through its gates was gone. In the four thousand years since Ur's golden age, relentless deposits of silt and clay carried down by the Tigris and Euphrates have pushed the Persian Gulf coastline nearly 150 kilometers farther south. When explorers first stood among Ur's desert ruins in 1815, they were astonished to find ancient seashells still resting against its walls.
