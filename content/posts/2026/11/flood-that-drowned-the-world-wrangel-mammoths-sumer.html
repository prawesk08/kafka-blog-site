The flood that drowned the world after the Ice Age, the first civilization, and eight mammoths who kept their kind alive for six thousand years on a small Arctic island.

As the seas rose at the end of the Ice Age, the world’s last mammoths, trapped on their way to Alaska, kept their kind alive on one small island for the next six thousand years. The same water had turned the people who once made their homes where the Persian Gulf is today into refugees.

In one corner of the world, a small herd of mammoths was stranded on an island. In another, the first human refugees from the water dug canals and gave birth to the world’s first civilization.

In short

  • As the Ice Age ended, the ice melted and the world’s seas rose by about 120 meters.
  • Beringia, the grassland bridge between Siberia and Alaska, drowned about 11,000 years ago. People, mammoths, camels and horses had all crossed it.
  • About eight mammoths were trapped on Wrangel Island. Their descendants outlived the mainland mammoths by some six thousand years, until around 1750 BCE.
  • The same water drowned the Ur-Shatt Valley, where the Persian Gulf is now, pushing its people north into southern Mesopotamia.
  • There, refugees from the south met Ubaid farmers from the north. Canals, administration and the first city, Eridu, followed.

The world of twenty thousand years ago was not like today's world.

When the ice began to melt twenty thousand years ago, the whole world changed.

The melting came to everyone alike.

When the Ice Melted, Twenty Thousand Years Ago

At the peak of the Ice Age, much of the northern hemisphere lay buried under ice. Canada, Scandinavia and northern Europe were covered by sheets of ice up to two kilometers thick.

But the ice was not everywhere. Cold alone is not enough for ice to build up; it also needs snow. Siberia was cold, but extremely dry. So even at the height of the Ice Age, no ice sheet ever formed there.

Instead, a vast open plain stretched to the south and east, from Spain through Siberia all the way to Alaska. We know it as Beringia.

It was not an empty place. For thousands of years, this open plain was the playground of a giant: the woolly mammoth. If you have seen the film Ice Age, its hero, Manfred, or Manny, is a woolly mammoth.

Beringia, the mammoths’ playground (image from the internet).

This ice began to melt twenty thousand years ago. The meltwater had to go somewhere. And it went into the sea. After the end of the Ice Age, sea levels around the world rose by a hundred and twenty meters, and the rise went on until about 6,000 years ago.

The sea did not rise everywhere at once. The first place it touched was that same grassland joining Siberia and Alaska.

The Busiest Road on Earth, Joining Asia and America

For most of the Ice Age, Siberia and Alaska were joined. Today that land lies under the sea, but in the Ice Age it was dry ground. Mammoths used this route to move back and forth.

This route joining Asia and America was not the narrow strip we imagine. The “bridge” was a vast grassland. In places more than a thousand kilometers wide, it was grazed by herds of mammoth, bison and wild horses.

An artist’s impression of a woolly mammoth (digital image from the internet).

And Ice Age people followed those herds onto the open plain, about 60,000 years ago. We often imagine bands of hunters trailing mammoth herds from Asia into America. But the story of how people reached America is not that simple.

For their first thousands of years there, the Ice Age hunters stayed in Siberia and Beringia. Ice blocked the way into Canada, and they could go nowhere until it opened. Only after the ice melted and the way opened did they reach America, about sixteen thousand years ago. Within a few thousand years, they had reached the southern tip of the Americas.

Beringia had connected Asia and America for hundreds of thousands of years, long before mammoths or Homo sapiens existed.

Camels and Horses: Born in America, Raised in Asia

Beringia wasn't just a one-way route for migration from Siberia to America. Many species that first evolved in America crossed the same route into Asia, Eurasia, and Africa, where they continued evolving as natives.

The desert camel is the best example. It may sound surprising, but the camel is an American animal. It evolved in North America and spent its first forty million years there.

Some of the camel’s ancestors crossed Beringia, heading west, and reached Asia. The Bactrian camels of Central Asia and the camels of the Arabian Desert are their descendants. Some went south and became llamas, alpacas, and their kin. Those that stayed behind in America all died out about twelve thousand years ago.

In other words, the Arabian camel, the animal that made civilization’s first long journeys possible, was itself an immigrant that came from America to Asia at the end of the Ice Age.

The horse’s story is much the same. The horse also evolved in America. Horses crossed Beringia into Asia, spread out across Central Asia, and died out in their own homeland. America’s native horses vanished about ten thousand years ago, at the end of the Ice Age. For horses to return, America had to wait eight thousand years for Columbus and the other Spanish ships.

For hundreds of thousands of years, the Beringian bridge was the busiest road on Earth. Mammoths and people heading east, camels and horses heading west. Then the ice melted and came as water. Slowly at first, very slowly, and then, from about 14,000 years ago, all at once.

Beringia was low-lying, and the sea drowns low ground first. First the coast went under. Then the plain narrowed. Then it broke into pieces. About 11,000 years ago the bridge disappeared completely, and Siberia and Alaska were separated for good.

A bridge that had joined Asia and America for hundreds of thousands of years sank beneath the sea within a few thousand.

When the Grass Ran Out on the Siberian Steppe

In the last ten thousand years of the Ice Age, not only the sea rose.

First, the world itself warmed. The world of ten thousand years ago was about five degrees Celsius warmer than the world of twenty thousand years ago. For the mammoths enjoying their cold Siberian playground, it proved to be a death sentence.

As temperatures rose, the air grew more humid, the frozen ground thawed, and water with nowhere to drain turned into a bog. Where grass had grown for thousands of years, scrub and moss now sprang up, along with new bushes and birch trees never seen there before.

An adult mammoth needs about two hundred kilograms of grass a day, and that much grass could no longer be found anywhere. As the grasslands shrank, the herds broke up into small groups. We hunters added to the pressure. The thawing permafrost may also have spread new diseases.

Whatever the causes, the result was the same. The last mammoths in Siberia drew their final breaths about ten thousand years ago.

But eight mammoths stranded on a small island in Beringia kept their kind alive for another six thousand years, far from the rest of the world.

The Mammoths’ Last Home: Wrangel Island

At the northern edge of Beringia there is a patch of high ground. As the water rose, the plain around it drowned, and that ridge became an island above the water.

About 10,000 years ago, some eight mammoths were cut off from the Siberian mainland on this island. They had walked there before the ridge became an island, and then, finding no way back, they stayed.

Today we know it as Wrangel Island. The water trapped them, and the very island that trapped them kept them alive.

The water trapped eight woolly mammoths, and the very island that trapped them kept them alive for another six thousand years.

Mammoths that had vanished everywhere else in the world survived only on this island. The herd grew from eight animals to as many as eight hundred at one point. For thousands of years, their numbers averaged around four hundred.

They had not chosen the island. The sea had stopped them there. There was no way out. The land was what it was. They could eat only as much grass as grew. There was no way to get more.

The same grass, the same rocks, generation after generation; for six thousand years they waited.

Wrangel Island (image from the internet).

While the mammoths lived on their island, people in another corner of the world were being driven out by the same water. The people who reached the southern plains of Mesopotamia around 6,500 BCE—and who would later build the world’s first civilization—were refugees chased away by the rising sea, by this first flood.

The Fertile Valley That Drowned at the Other End of the World

Where the Persian Gulf lies today, a green, fertile valley once stood. The sea where the Tigris and Euphrates now meet did not exist during the Ice Age. The two rivers met and continued south as a single river for about another thousand kilometers, passing through the narrow mouth of Hormuz before emptying into the Gulf of Oman. This Ice Age river is called the Ur-Shatt.

The fertile valley that drowned to form the Persian Gulf (image from the internet).

When the sea stood a hundred and twenty meters lower than it does today, this valley was home to ancient people. It was very different from the surrounding land and far more fertile. To the east lay the dry mountains of Iran; to the west, the Arabian Desert; and between them lay this green plain, built from the soil the river brought down.

Of the people who left Africa and spread across the world via the Red Sea, one group made this valley its home about sixty thousand years ago. There were lakes on both sides of the river. There were marshes. Reeds grew. There were date palms. The river had fish, the marshes had birds, and animals came to the banks to drink.

Life was not hard. No one had to go far for food. You could catch fish, trap birds, eat marsh roots and tender reed shoots. The dates grew by themselves. The animals came to drink on their own.

For thousands of years, people stayed right there. The same river, the same marsh, the same dates. Generation after generation.

But the same water that drowned Beringia was now about to arrive here too.

An Apocalypse Within a Single Generation

At first, the sea rose slowly, about a centimeter a year. Around 14,000 years ago, the rate tripled.

Little by little, the water swallowed the Ur-Shatt Valley. The valley was extremely flat, and on flat land water does not rise upward; it spreads sideways.

In this valley built by the river, the sea began to move inland a meter every three days—about a hundred and twenty meters a year. Do the arithmetic, and that’s a kilometer every ten years. A thousand kilometers in eight thousand years.

In other words, for the first time, a person could watch the flood eat away at the land within a single lifetime.

The rock a child had stood on to fish would be underwater by the time he was grown. Paths turned into marsh. The date palms that survived withered in salt water. Every generation had to move a little higher and relocate its homes. A few generations later, their descendants had to do it all over again.

The rock a child had stood on to fish would be underwater by the time he was grown.

And the valley kept narrowing. People who had spread across it for thousands of years were now crowded onto shrinking land. One day, even that narrow strip disappeared. The water completely drowned the Ur-Shatt Valley.

By around 6,000 BCE, the Persian Gulf had already formed, and one high piece of land left above it survives today as Bahrain.

The First Story of a Flood That Drowned the World

The story of the people of Sumer begins with this very flood. Their myths say that the world of their ancestors was one day drowned by waters that brought a great deluge.

They called present-day Bahrain Dilmun, their ancestral land. A clean, pure land where no one fell sick and no one grew old. It was there that Enki first made humankind.

One day, the gods of this garden paradise grew angry and decided to wipe out the people around them. But Enki, the god of fresh water, disagreed. He taught the people there how to escape.

“Tear down your house, build a boat,” Enki told Ziusudra.

Then the water came. Seven days and seven nights. Everything was gone. Only Ziusudra’s boat survived.

This story of a great flood drowning the world was later recorded in Babylon as the flood of Utnapishtim. Then it appeared as Noah’s flood in the Hebrew Bible, and later in the Christian Bible and the Muslim Quran, spreading across the world.

The same story echoes in our own Samudra Manthan and in the Matsya avatar. It may be the oldest story humans have kept telling. But the deluge their ancestors lived through was something else altogether.

A seven-day flood on the rivers of Mesopotamia is nothing new. But the water their ancestors faced did not come for just seven days. It came over thousands of years, and it never stopped. Fathers moved on from where their grandfathers had left off, and sons from where their fathers had left off. For hundreds of generations, every generation saw the same thing: The water rises, the land shrinks, and we must move north.

The water rises, the land shrinks, we must move north.

The rising sea drowned marshes, lakes, and forests. It swallowed whole villages. People were crowded into ever-narrower spaces, and before the sea could take its next step, they moved from one place to the next, learning to live in each new place.

This migration north in search of higher ground continued from 6,000 to 5,500 BCE. Carrying the story of their world lost beneath the water, the people of Sumer first reached the coasts of southern Iraq.

The World’s First Civilization, Born in the Least Likely Place on Earth

But southern Iraq was no paradise when they arrived. It was perhaps the least suitable place in the world for the first civilization to begin. There was no stone, no forests to cut for timber, no mines, and no metal anywhere—only a blazing sun, hot winds blowing wildly, and sandy marshes into which your feet sank.

But there were two rivers, and here they worked like an elixir of life. Flowing down from the north with soil from Turkey’s Taurus Mountains, these rivers kept Mesopotamia's riverbanks fertile for hundreds of thousands of years.

Around those very mountains in Turkey, whose soil the Tigris and Euphrates carried downstream, people first developed farming more than ten thousand years ago.

The Ubaidas, Who Followed the Rivers from Turkey to Mesopotamia

Former hunters who had only just learned to farm began spreading everywhere in search of farmland. Around 6,500 BCE, some bold people from the early farming Samarra culture and the northern foothills followed the rivers south. Archaeologists call this time the Ubaid period.

As they moved downriver, they made the river their companion. Where there were no trees, they cut the tall marsh reeds and bound them into huts. In the shade of the date palms by the river, they began planting pomegranates and figs. Later, they made the potter’s wheel and learned to work clay with it.

But those early people of the Ubaid still lived at nature’s mercy. They were limited to wherever the river reached. They had not yet learned to build fields or boats.

Sumer and Ubaid: Two Worlds Meet

These people coming down from the north met the refugees coming up from the south. Two groups, from two different worlds. And water had brought both there.

We call them the Ubaid civilization because archaeologists found their pottery at a place called Ubaid. But who they were and where they came from are still unclear. Take this as an example: Homo sapiens reached Egypt some twenty thousand years after reaching Nepal.

The Ubaid people who came down from the north were people of the soil. They knew when to sow, when to harvest, and how to store grain after harvest. They had sickles; they had pots.

Those who came from the south were people of the water. The marsh was nothing new to them. Where fish get caught in a net, when to cut the reeds, which roots are good to eat, and which month the birds arrive—no one had to teach them these things. Most important of all, they remembered how water rises, where it goes, and where it stops. We call them the Sumerians.

Had these two kinds of knowledge stayed apart in southern Iraq, perhaps neither would have amounted to much.

The group from the north brought farming; the group from the south knew how to build canals, and together they carried farming across the desert of southern Mesopotamia.

These two communities, brought together after the Ice Age, started the world’s first civilization. Begun in the barren desert of southern Iraq by the people of Sumer and Ubaid (later known as the Akkadians), this civilization went on to rule that part of Mesopotamia in one form or another for the next four thousand years and taught humankind the ABCs of civilization.

That beginning of civilization came from turning streams into canals and stretching farmland into the desert.

Administration, Born from Turning Rivers into Canals

Farming grew, and villages grew bigger. Officials were needed to look after the canals linking the rivers. Someone had to assign the officials; someone had to give the orders. The workers digging the canals had to be paid.

So, the need to dig canals and manage water gave birth to the world’s first administration.

Through canals, villages and administration, the world’s first city was born here too. At Eridu, in today’s southern Iraq, some 10,000 people lived in 5,400 BCE. This may well be the world’s first city.

Ruins of the main temple at Eridu (photo from the internet).

Born in the marshes between two rivers, Sumerian civilization endured in Mesopotamia for about four thousand years. Over those four thousand years, it changed humankind forever. The Sumerians laid the foundations for the bread we eat, the beer we drink, the arithmetic we use, and the cities we live in.

The End of Sumer

But the cycle of time and nature is merciless. The very soil and water that gave Sumer life eventually turned against it: once the water stopped coming, the salt left in the soil and desert sand poisoned their civilization.

Sometime around 1900 BCE, after the Elamites burned Ur, Sumerian civilization ended forever. After nearly four thousand years of glorious history, it vanished completely. The people who gave the world writing were themselves wiped entirely from human memory.

In that same place, around 1,700 BCE, the kingdoms of Babylon rose, and Hammurabi was born. By then, the kings of Sumer had already become legendary figures.

About three hundred years later, Sumerian civilization had vanished from memory itself. Only the name of Sargon the Great of Akkad would survive, and the kings of Sumer were already just gods of ancient times.

The Last Breath of the Ice Age Refugees

Far from southern Iraq, in another corner of the Earth, a different wait was going on.

Those last mammoths, imprisoned by the sea, were still alive. For six thousand years, on the same narrow island, among the same grass and rocks, they had lived on, generation after generation.

When the Sumerians built the world’s first city, those mammoths were there. And when Sumerian civilization collapsed completely and disappeared into the depths of history, they were still there, breathing.

About three hundred years after the first great civilization disappeared into the dust, around 1750 BCE, the world’s last mammoth breathed its last in the cold wind of Wrangel Island.

One group of refugees left after ushering in a new age of human history; the other took an entire Ice Age with it.

This was the story of the greatest flood the human world has ever seen. We have had to see with our own eyes the unimaginable damage melting ice can cause. The world’s temperature is rising every day, and in the Himalayas, it is rising twice as fast. What will happen if the ice remaining on Earth’s third pole melts? I hope that, starting now, we can prepare to prevent the loss of lives and, as far as we can, property too.

Frequently asked questions

Why did mammoths survive so long on Wrangel Island?

Rising seas cut Wrangel off from Siberia about 10,000 years ago. No hunters reached it, and a small herd lived on the island’s grass for some six thousand years. The last mammoths died about 4,000 years ago.

What was Beringia?

The land exposed between Siberia and Alaska when Ice Age seas were much lower. This grassland, in places more than a thousand kilometers wide, drowned about 11,000 years ago and became the Bering Strait.

Did a flood really fill the Persian Gulf?

Yes. In the Ice Age, most of the Gulf was dry land, crossed by the combined Tigris and Euphrates. Between about 14,000 and 6,000 years ago, the sea slowly filled it. That this inspired the flood myths is a hypothesis, not a proven fact.

Where did the Sumerians come from?

It is still debated. One hypothesis says people displaced from the drowning Gulf moved north and became the Sumerians. Other scholars think Sumer grew out of the local Ubaid-period population.

What was the world’s first city?

Many consider Eridu the first city. Settled around 5400 BCE, it was also where, according to the Sumerians themselves, kingship first came down to earth.
