A gecko makes a copy of its tail. The thakthake, the salamander of Nepal’s eastern hills, can rebuild a severed leg just as it was, with bone, nerves and toes. The same instructions are in our DNA too, but after birth they are locked away.

How the thakthake regrows a severed leg, at a glance.

How I Met the Salamander at School

I first came across the salamander as a child. It was during my school days: on the break after my sixth-grade exams, I started rummaging through the bookshelf at home. That was when I found an old science magazine in Hindi.

I don’t remember the magazine’s name; I’ve forgotten the title of the story and the hero’s name, too. It was about a scientist who had lost his arms and legs in an accident and experimented on himself, using the salamander’s ability to regrow lost body parts. I don’t even remember whether the biologist succeeded or failed in the end. But that is where my bond with the salamander began.

It was this astonishing ability of the salamander that awakened in me the urge to study biology. For a long time after reading the story, I assumed that salamanders regrowing severed limbs was just the writer’s imagination. Only when I studied salamanders in plus-two did I learn that they really do have an astonishing power to heal. Cut off a salamander’s arm, leg, tail, or other body part, and within a few months it can grow back just as it was.

Transferring that ability from the salamander to a human, as in the story, really is just imagination, like that Lizard villain from Spider-Man.

The Salamander and the House Gecko Are Not the Same

Until I finished school, I believed the salamander was a cousin of the house gecko; after all, the gecko grows a new tail, too. But far from being cousins, the two don’t even belong to the same animal class.

House geckos and lizards are reptiles: they have dry, scaly skin, lay eggs, and are born and grow up on land. The salamander is a relative of the frog, an amphibian: it has slippery skin, lays its eggs in water, and its young grow up in water like tadpoles.

That both the salamander and the gecko can regrow a lost tail isn’t because they are related; it’s a coincidence arrived at by two different paths. And the tail a gecko grows back isn’t its old tail either, but more on that later.

The Salamander, or Maipokhari’s Thakthake

Only much later did I learn that the salamander was not just a creature in some faraway book. It is found in great numbers in Maipokhari, in our own Ilam. It is about twenty centimeters long, with rough black skin and orange markings. Its head resembles a crocodile’s, with bony ridges along its flanks. Because its head and body look so much like a crocodile’s, the thakthake is also called the crocodile newt.

There are about eight hundred species of salamander worldwide. The newt, the salamander found in Europe and most studied, looks much like ours. But there are many other kinds of salamander: the axolotl of Mexico lives its whole life in its juvenile form. The giant salamanders of Japan and China grow up to one and a half meters long; they are the largest amphibians in the world. Maipokhari’s thakthake is a relative of all of them, and it has the same ability.

Locals in Nepal’s Ilam and Panchthar, in Darjeeling, and around Bhutan already knew this salamander as the thakthake. But only ten years ago did we learn that our thakthake is not the same as the salamander found in Burma and China (Tylototriton verrucosus).

In July 2014, Janak Raj Khatiwada, Shanta Paudel, and Subarna Ghimire collected a few specimens from Maipokhari. Comparing their morphology and DNA confirmed that this was a salamander species new to science. In 2015, Khatiwada’s team introduced it to the world as Tylototriton himalayanus.

Maipokhari’s thakthake is found only in Ilam and Panchthar, Darjeeling, and western Bhutan, at elevations of roughly nine hundred to two thousand six hundred meters. It is also the only salamander species found in Nepal.

Does a Gecko Grow Back the Same Tail?

A gecko’s tail doesn’t break off by itself; the gecko drops it. If a cat chases it or someone grabs its tail, it contracts its muscles and simply leaves the tail behind. This is where the difference between the salamander and the gecko begins.

A gecko’s tail doesn’t break just anywhere. A weak line is already built into the tail’s bones, and the tail breaks only along that line. The blood vessels also have points that can be sealed shut. As soon as the tail comes off, those points seal and block the flow of blood, so the gecko doesn’t bleed when it drops its tail.

The dropped tail thrashes on the ground for a few minutes. Even after it separates from the body, its nerves and muscles continue to work for a while. This draws the predator’s attention, and the gecko escapes.

The new tail is not the old one; it is only a copy. The old bone inside the tail doesn’t return. Instead of bone, a long tube of cartilage forms. The joint knobs don’t form as before, and the spinal nerve doesn’t fully return. You can tell just by looking: the scales are a different color, and the new tail is shorter than before. And a gecko can never drop its tail a second time from the same spot. The weak line where the tail breaks exists only in bone, not in the new cartilage tube.

The only Nepali creature with the ability I read about still lives in Maipokhari, Ilam. The thakthake, which still knows how to read the instructions we have forgotten, must surely have come down to the pond again this year.

The gecko makes something else that looks like a tail; the salamander restores the original structure of its cells.

What Happens When We Get Hurt?

A week ago, while opening a carton of supplies that had arrived at the lab, I nicked myself with a paper knife. The blood from the cut above the nail of my index finger stopped within a few minutes, but the mark is still visible.

When my finger was cut, the body first tried to stop the bleeding. Then immune cells arrived to clear dirt and germs from around the wound. Next, fibroblasts began weaving collagen threads to close the wound. A few days later, the weave tightened and the wound closed, but a scar remained where it had been cut.

When we are wounded, the newly formed scar can never become like the skin it once was. In healthy skin, the collagen mesh spreads evenly in every direction, woven like a spider’s web or a fishing net. When a wound is patched, new collagen is added, but it is not arranged as before. In the rush to close the wound, collagen piles up everywhere without structure. Hair doesn’t grow on a scar, it doesn’t sweat, and you can’t even feel it when it’s touched.

There is a reason the body chooses this route. Rebuilding skin to its original state takes weeks, and for all those days there is a risk of infection. Even so, we haven’t entirely lost the salamander’s kind of healing. Even if two-thirds of the liver is removed, the remaining tissue grows back to its original weight within a few weeks, though not to its original shape. If a small child’s fingertip is cut off above the base of the nail, it can grow back, bone, skin, and nail. And if an embryo is wounded in the first months in the mother’s womb, the wound heals without a scar.

It isn’t that we never had the salamander’s ability. As we grow up, this ability gradually fades away; the salamander retains it throughout its life.

From One Cell to a Whole Body

Every animal that reproduces sexually, like us, begins as a single cell. That cell divides into two, then four, then eight. Within a few days, a small ball of cells forms: the morula. Up to this point, all the cells are alike. None of them yet knows what it will become; each can become anything.

As the morula continues dividing, it first becomes a blastula. The cells now begin to determine what they will become. As the embryo grows, three layers separate. The outermost layer becomes skin and nerves. The middle layer becomes bone, muscle, heart, and blood. The inner layer becomes the gut, lungs, and liver. The cells that build arms and legs come from the middle layer.

In most animals, once cells have chosen what to become, there is no going back; once a cell has become a heart cell, it can never become a skin cell again.

How Does a Salamander Regrow a Leg?

When we are wounded, our bodies rush to close the wound by producing as much collagen as possible. The salamander does exactly the opposite.

When a salamander’s leg is cut off, within the first few minutes, skin cells begin moving in from the edges. Within twelve hours, a thin layer has formed over the wound. No blood clots into a scab as it does in us; from the start, the wound is covered by skin-like cells. The newly formed cells over the stump continue to send chemical signals to the fibroblasts underneath. The rush to patch the wound with a jumbled mesh of collagen, as happens in humans, never even gets started.

An even more astonishing change occurs in the cells around the wound. The nearby muscle, bone, and cartilage give up their identity. They forget they are muscle and revert to an embryonic state. The cells then gather at the wound to form a small mound: the blastema. Here, the salamander defies the established rule that once the three layers separate during embryonic development, there is no going back.

For a salamander to form a blastema, blood vessels are needed; without immune cells, the healing process cannot even begin. Even after the injury, the blood vessels continue to deliver the same chemicals to the salamander as before. These chemicals tell the cells of the newly formed blastema to keep dividing.

If the nerves are severed along with the salamander’s leg, none of this process can begin. Not every blastema cell can become anything, though; the old cells can only revert to their former role in their former place. A cell that came from muscle becomes muscle again, one from cartilage becomes cartilage. Skin becomes skin, nerve becomes nerve.

As the blastema grows, the work left off in the embryo resumes. The blastema reactivates the leg-building genes. The salamander hasn’t found a new method; it has simply started reading its own old instructions again, just as before. As in the embryo, the cells divide again: some become bone, some muscle, some nerve, some skin.

The salamander hasn’t found a new method; it has simply started reading its own old instructions again.

And these cells also know where they are in the leg. As a leg forms in the embryo, every cell carries its own address: how far it is from the shoulder. That address isn’t erased even after the animal grows up. The blastema has no picture of the leg it must build, only addresses. The cells at the cut read their address and then build whatever is missing below it. That is why a cut at the wrist grows back only a hand, while a cut at the shoulder grows back the whole leg from the shoulder down.

It takes a salamander one or two months to regrow a leg, and a large salamander even longer. At first, the new leg is smaller than the old one, then it slowly grows to full size. The new leg isn’t a copy like the gecko’s tail: bone where bone should be, joints where joints should be, and the same number of fingers as before.

But even the salamander’s regenerative ability has limits. It rebuilds only outward from the cut: legs, tails, and jaws regrow. If the same spot is cut repeatedly, the new leg slowly stops forming as before: fewer fingers, sometimes crooked. Salamanders can heal injuries to the head or heart, but if the head or body is cut off, they die too.

Why Can’t Our Bodies Heal Like a Salamander’s?

Our blood is warm, and bacteria multiply quickly in that warmth. So the longer a wound stays open, the greater the risk. Our immune system is built with that in mind: as soon as we are injured, inflammation sets in, recruiting neutrophils and macrophages, and on their signal, fibroblasts rush to close the wound.

A salamander is cold-blooded and lives in cold water. In that environment, bacteria can’t multiply as quickly as they do in our bodies, so leaving a wound open for a few weeks isn’t as risky. When a salamander is injured, macrophages arrive first, but they rush to release chemicals that calm inflammation rather than to close the wound. The wound stays calm, and in that calm, the blastema forms and the wound begins to fill. In the lab, if macrophages are removed, salamanders scar just like we do.

Where Is That Locked-Away Key?

We have the genes salamanders need to heal, too. The genes switched on in the blastema aren’t new; salamanders didn’t invent them. When we were embryos, those same genes helped build our arms. As our bodies developed, they were simply switched off, bit by bit.

Every cell in our body carries the same DNA. A skin cell has the genes for building a heart; a heart cell has the genes for building skin. The difference lies not in which genes a cell carries, but in which are switched on and which are switched off.

Genes are switched off by chemical tags placed on a chromosome’s DNA. Whether small molecules called methyl are attached to the DNA determines which genes are off and which are on. Where there is a lot of methyl, the DNA is wound tightly around spools of protein called histones, and the gene-reading machinery can’t even reach it. Only genes in open stretches with few methyl molecules attached can be used to make RNA and protein.

In us, once the body is built, the embryonic genes are switched off in just this way. The methyl tags go on, the DNA winds up tight, and each cell gets on with its own job. Once genes are switched off, the tags are passed on through cell division to the next cell: skin stays skin, heart stays heart.

In us, this heavy padlock on those genes doesn’t unlock when we’re injured. Genes do switch on in our wounds, but they are genes for making collagen, not for building an arm.

In salamanders, these tags come off after an injury. Proteins that remove methyl groups become active in the wound cells, the tightly wound DNA begins to open, and genes that were switched off since the embryo are read again. This is what it means for a cell to give up its identity: the DNA hasn’t changed; the lock on it has been opened.

The DNA hasn’t changed; the lock on it has been opened.

The DNA is the same in both; the difference lies in which parts can be read. When we talk about this lock, we also have to look at the salamander’s genome.

A salamander’s DNA is ten times larger than ours. We have three billion nucleotides; the axolotl, the salamander of Mexico, has thirty-two billion. Its number of chromosomes, though, is smaller than ours: we have 46, it has 28. The axolotl’s 28 chromosomes are far larger than ours, yet its number of genes is about the same as ours. All the extra DNA sits between the genes, as long stretches of repeating letters. It is the DNA between the genes that decides which gene switches on when.

The axolotl’s genome is ten times larger than ours, yet it has just as many genes. And for all that DNA, the salamander has no magic gene we lack. The difference is that a huge genome means large cells, and large cells divide slowly. That is one reason it takes a salamander months to grow a new leg.

Because salamander cells divide less often, their risk of cancer is relatively low. So salamanders can afford to keep those genes unlocked, whereas ours are locked to guard against cancer. Both of us have the genes that switch on in the blastema; the difference lies in when they switch on and when they switch off.

So What Are Scientists Doing Now?

The axolotl’s full genome was sequenced only in 2018. Afterward, we learned how blastema cells remember their address: a certain protein is abundant in cells near the shoulder and scarce in cells near the fingers. In the lab, if that protein is boosted, a blastema that was about to grow just a hand starts growing a whole arm.

The salamander’s ability is being tested in mice as well. When a fingertip is cut off and a protein similar to the salamander’s is boosted there, part of it has grown back, bone and nail included, instead of scarring.

But we need to think carefully about transferring the salamander’s ability to humans. The very inflammation that is suppressed to prevent scarring is also what keeps infection at bay. And the very lock that is opened to make cells give up their identity, if left open, lets cells divide out of control and turn into cancer.

That is why today’s work is not aimed at regrowing an entire arm. Healing stubborn wounds in diabetic patients without scarring, restoring sweat glands to burned skin, and turning the scar left in the heart after a heart attack back into muscle: these are the small steps being taken.

And, That Story

Thinking about that story today, it seems to me it was a Spider-Man comic retold in Hindi. That was exactly what Spider-Man’s Dr. Curt Connors was after, too. Having lost an arm in an accident, he tried lizard genes on himself. The arm did grow back. But lizard genes give only what a lizard has: just as a gecko makes a copy of its tail, Connors got a copy of an arm and became the Lizard himself. What he needed was a salamander; what he chose was a lizard.

What he needed was a salamander; what he chose was a lizard.

When I pulled that magazine from the bookshelf during that sixth-grade break, I had no idea that the way wounds heal would become the very thing I study. I don’t know where that magazine is now.

But that creature, it turns out, never stayed inside a book. The only Nepali creature with the ability I read about in that story still lives in Maipokhari, Ilam. The thakthake, which still knows how to read the instructions we have forgotten, must surely have come down to the pond again this year after the first monsoon rains to lay its eggs.
