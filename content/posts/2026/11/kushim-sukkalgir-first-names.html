Before poetry, epics, and laws came accounting. And the numbers were already in our hands. Sumerian civilization, rising from Mesopotamia’s clay and riverside reeds, gave the world its first writing system and its oldest surviving literature.

The Oldest Name in History Belonged to a Man Counting Barley

This article was first published in Nepali on Shilapatra on August 22, 2026 (Bhadau 6, 2083 BS). This is the author’s own English translation.

Accounts before poetry: how writing began, at a glance (created with Claude).

Have you ever wondered what the oldest human names we have found are? We tend to imagine a queen or king, a god or goddess, or an extraordinary hero. But the oldest named woman and man known to history come from this very city, and they were two ordinary people.

Five and a half thousand years ago, Kushim counted barley in a storehouse in Uruk; a few generations later, Sukkalgir was written down as someone’s property.

This is their story.

In short

  • Writing began as accounting, not poetry: the oldest tablets from Uruk (c. 3400–3100 BCE) record barley, goats and beer.
  • The name Kushim appears on eighteen of those tablets. It may be the oldest name we can read, though it could be a job title rather than a person.
  • Sukkalgir, listed around 3100 BCE as one of two slaves owned by Gal-Sal, is the first woman whose name we can read.
  • The popular story of “Nisa”, Kushim’s female supervisor, has no scholarly basis.
  • Our sixty-second minute and 360-degree circle come from Sumerian counting in sixties, which you can do on your finger joints.

The oldest names in history belong to no king or god. Kushim counted barley in a storehouse in Uruk five and a half thousand years ago; Sukkalgir is the first woman whose name was ever written down, recorded as someone’s property. From counting to sixty on our fingers to clay tablets and Gilgamesh, this is the story of how writing began.

The Story of Gilgamesh, the World’s Oldest Epic

‘He who saw the deep, who knew the foundations of the earth, who was wise in all things.’

This is how the oldest literature of human civilization begins. The Epic of Gilgamesh, written in Uruk around 2100 BCE, tells the story of Gilgamesh, king of Uruk. Written nearly a thousand years before the oldest parts of the Bible, Gilgamesh tells stories of kingship, grief, immortality, and a flood that drowned the whole world.

The story of Gilgamesh is not a story of victory. It is the story of a king who set out to conquer death and, in the end, came home to his own city, exhausted.

The Epic of Gilgamesh, the flood story (1200 BCE, Assyrian tablet, British Museum).

According to the story, Gilgamesh was two parts god and one part man, carrying both a god's restlessness and a man's weakness. He knew that he would die. And so, he does what people afraid of death do.

Gilgamesh cuts through the cedar forest the gods had forbidden anyone to enter. He fights a monster. He crosses the waters of death. While he sleeps on a riverbank, a snake steals the immortality he was about to win. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk empty-handed. And finally, standing before the walls of his own city, he says:

‘Behold the walls of Uruk, climb up and walk on them. Examine the foundation, inspect the brickwork. Is it not made of kiln-fired brick? Did not the gods themselves lay out its plan?’

– The Epic of Gilgamesh (1200 BCE, Assyrian tablet)

This is how the world’s oldest story ends. Not with the gods pleased, not with a throne regained. A tired king, the walls he built himself, and a quiet understanding: the city I built will outlive me by far.

Gilgamesh stood before the walls of Uruk sometime around 2700 BCE. The walls and stones where he stood crumbled into ruins over time. The city was erased from memory, but what it gave human civilization is far greater than Gilgamesh’s walls of stone and mud.

Five hundred years before Gilgamesh, Uruk already stood in southern Mesopotamia as the world’s first great city. Two hundred years before that, someone in Uruk began patting wet clay flat in their palm, pressing a piece of reed into it, and making marks. Marks that would last far longer than stone and walls.

A stroke. A number. A word.

Numbers Before Words

Before Gilgamesh, before the cedar forest and the walls of Uruk, long before every story we have heard about the ancient world, numbers came first. And no one had to invent numbers. They were already in our bodies, hiding in the fingers of our hands.

Raise your right hand and spread it open. With your thumb, touch and count the joints of the other four fingers. Each finger has three joints. Four fingers, three joints. Without lifting the other hand, one hand gets you to twelve.

Once you reach twelve, fold down one finger of your left hand. Each time you reach twelve, fold one finger. When all five fingers are folded, how far have you counted?

Five times twelve. Sixty.

This is how it began. Not in a palace or a temple, but in the hands of ordinary people who had to keep everyday accounts. No one announced this system of counting in sixties. It was already in all our bodies: anyone, anywhere, even someone who knew nothing else, could count on their fingers.

The counting system begun by Mesopotamian civilization (created with Claude).

This is how the city of Uruk was built in Mesopotamia; this is where Sumerian civilization began. Those numbers are still with us. The Sumerians didn’t just begin writing; they buried the counting they began so deep in the bones of civilization that we still haven’t let go of it.

We still use that mark for five when tallying: four strokes struck through the middle. Sixty seconds in a minute. Sixty minutes in an hour. Three hundred and sixty degrees in a circle. The clock hanging on the wall still tells time the way the people of Sumer counted on their fingers five thousand years ago.

But sixty is not writing. Sixty is counting. And for a very long time, hundreds, perhaps thousands of years, counting was enough. Writing came much later. And it began not with words but with pictures.

Clay, Reeds and the First Marks

The first stars of human civilization began to shine in Uruk around 3400 BCE. Another Sumerian poem written a little after the Gilgamesh stories, Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta, credits the invention of writing to the king of Uruk (3400 BCE), Kullaba, who had more messages than a messenger could carry.

‘The messenger’s mouth was heavy, and he could not repeat the message. Then Kullaba patted some clay into a tablet and set words upon it. Before Kullaba did so, there had been no custom of setting words on clay.’

– Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta (c. 2000 BCE)

The people of Sumer had plenty of two things: the river mud beneath their feet and the reeds growing on its banks. A scribe would pat a lump of clay into a tablet, much like today’s mobile phone, cut a reed into a point, and press that point into the clay to make marks. The wedge shape gave the world’s oldest script its name: cuneiform, meaning wedge-shaped.

From pictures to wedges: samples of the earliest signs (created with Claude).

The first marks were pictographs, simple pictures anyone could understand by looking. A picture of a fish meant fish. A drawn circle meant cattle. A jar meant oil. This system, which first appeared around 3400 BCE, is called proto-cuneiform.

The first records are dry: lists of goats, measures of barley, rations of beer. But their meaning was enormous. For the first time, writing freed information from depending on memory alone, moving it onto clay tablets that could travel anywhere. The first letters were not written for the gods. Nor for kings. They were written for grain.

In the Mesopotamian heat, clay dried hard and stayed that way. That is why those first marks survived, piled in old storerooms, buried under centuries of ruins. When archaeologists dug up tablets written in Uruk at that time, five thousand years later, they could still read the records.

Eighteen Surviving Tablets, and One Name

In that memory, eighteen tablets have survived. Just eighteen tablets recovered from the ruins of Uruk, inscribed sometime between about 3400 and 3100 BCE.

They were not laws. They were not hymns. Nor were they royal proclamations carved to last forever. They are receipts. Ledgers. The daily account books of a busy storehouse preserved for five thousand years. And on all eighteen tablets, the same two signs are inscribed: ‘Ku.’ ‘Shim.’

Kushim. The oldest name we find in history.

Kushim was not a king, a warrior, a priest, or even a mason who built walls. Kushim was an ordinary man who kept the temple’s accounts: a storekeeper standing at the storehouse door, counting what came in and what went out. His name repeats on all eighteen tablets in the same neat hand.

The first letters were not written for the gods. Nor for kings. They were written for grain. In his famous book Sapiens, Yuval Noah Harari reads one of the oldest messages our ancestors left behind like this:

‘29,086 measures barley, 37 months, Kushim.’

Probable meaning: ‘A total of 29,086 measures of barley were received over the course of 37 months. Signed, Kushim.’

Harari adds: ‘The earliest texts of history contain no philosophical insights, no poetry, legends, laws, or even royal triumphs. They are humdrum economic documents, recording the payment of taxes, the accumulation of debts and the ownership of property.’

Kushim’s tablet, Uruk, 3400–3000 BCE. Photo: Schøyen Collection MS 1717.

Even so, saying this much goes beyond what we can prove. There is almost no context around those signs, and it is still not settled whether Ku-Shim refers to a person, a title, or a whole institution. Kushim may have been a man. Kushim may have been a post.

One of those eighteen tablets may preserve history’s very first accounting error. The barley total is wrong, off by fifteen measures, and the mistake is found hastily corrected on the back of the tablet.

That mistake, buried for five thousand years, does what no monument can. It makes Kushim a person like us. Someone we recognize. Not a legend, just an ordinary man who got his sums slightly wrong in the rush of work.

Kushim wasn’t writing history. He was counting barley, doing his job.

The First Woman Written into History

Another tablet from a generation or two after Kushim’s dates to about 3100 BCE. It records a master and two slaves he bought.

Two slaves of Gal-Sal: En-pap X and Sukkalgir.

Three people in four words: a master, and the two people he enslaved. Part of En-pap’s name is broken off, and we still can’t read it. Only half his name survives. Linked with his is another name, Sukkalgir, which is beyond doubt.

In the archaeological record so far, she is the first woman whose name we can read. Not a goddess. Not a queen. Not a priestess carved on a temple wall.

Property.

Two slaves of Gal-Sal. Oriental Institute Museum, Chicago, OIM A2513 (photo: Jean Grant).

This is what the record gives us, and it is worth pausing on. The first time a woman’s name was written down anywhere in the world, it was written to keep account of whose property she was.

She didn’t write it. She had no say in it. What she was like, how she came to belong to someone else, how long she lived, we don’t know. We know her name, and we know that Gal-Sal had enslaved her.

And Then, About Nisa

In many places that tell the story of the first people in history, you will find Nisa named as the first woman: Kushim’s supervisor, the woman who signed his ledgers, the first woman whose name was written down in history.

It’s a good story. But there is no scholarship beneath it. Wherever she appears, readers usually guess the sign might be ‘Nissa,’ not Nisa. No one has evidence she was a woman at all. Somewhere between one telling and the next, she seems to have been invented.

The truth is harder. It usually is.

From Pictures to Letters

Civilization in Sumer was only just beginning. By 3000 BCE, Uruk had already become the largest city of the ancient world, home to some 50,000 to 100,000 permanent residents.

Within a few generations of Kushim, those pictures had begun to change. Slowly the pictographs grew simpler. Need slowly turned the pictures etched into clay tablets into letters.

In the Mesopotamian heat, a scribe copying hundreds of documents a day has no time to draw a fish every time he needs to say fish. The picture gets simpler. The sign rotates. An ox’s head turns into a few strokes that no one would recognize as an ox.

By 3000 BCE, the script had moved entirely away from pictures to wedge-shaped impressions pressed into clay at an angle, what we now call cuneiform. The number of signs fell from more than fifteen hundred to about six hundred.

When Letters Began to Carry Sounds

Then someone realized: what if the signs pressed into clay stood for sounds? The picture of a fish standing not for a fish, but for the sound of the word ‘fish.’ The picture of a head standing not for a head, but for the syllable that word makes.

And if a sign could stand for a sound, it could be used for any word with that sound. And if signs could represent any word, then written tablets could carry any idea at all.

But this novelty brought something else with it: narrowing access. Only a class of trained scribes could read and write these new signs. A profession was born. A quiet hierarchy settled around the written word, and the world was never the same again.

Because, for the first time, knowledge outlived speech and memory. The clay dried, and the letters survived. That is why we can still read their epics and hear the echo of their music. People could learn from kings and writers who had died hundreds of years before they were born. Authority could be handed down not only by speaking but in writing. Knowledge no longer had to be kept in memory alone; it could be built upon.

Sumer’s technology was only just beginning. This was the starting point of the human civilization that spread across Mesopotamia.

In the End, the Same Walls Again

A few centuries after Kushim got his barley sums wrong, scribes in the same city pressed the story of a king into clay. A king who went to the edge of the world and came back empty-handed. Who stood before his own walls and understood his own impermanence.

Kushim’s barley was eaten long ago. Uruk’s walls are now only ruins. Gilgamesh has been dead for five thousand years. But their names remain. The Epic of Gilgamesh was written six hundred years after Gilgamesh died, and a thousand years later, the same story gave birth to the Torah, the Bible, and the Quran.

And among the earliest names we have found is a woman’s name too: Sukkalgir.

The first time a woman’s name was written anywhere in the world, she was recorded as someone’s property. She didn’t write it. She had no say in it. A claim in a bill of sale: property, nothing more.

Five thousand years later, we can still read Kushim, En-pap and Sukkalgir. A man who counted barley. A man of whose name only half survives. And a woman whose name was written down by someone counting his property.

That is what writing did: it preserved memory. It preserved not only the powerful, but also the careful. And it preserved the powerless too.

Frequently asked questions

What is the oldest name in recorded history?

Probably Kushim. The name appears on eighteen accounting tablets from Uruk dated between about 3400 and 3100 BCE. Scholars are still not sure whether it names a person or an office.

Who is the first named woman in history?

Sukkalgir. A tablet from around 3100 BCE records her as one of two slaves owned by a man called Gal-Sal.

Why are there 60 minutes in an hour?

The Sumerians counted in sixties. Counting the 12 finger joints of one hand, including the thumb, and marking each dozen on the five fingers of the other brings you to sixty. The system passed through Babylon to our clocks and circles.

When was cuneiform invented?

Picture-writing (proto-cuneiform) began in Uruk around 3400 BCE. By about 3000 BCE, those pictures had become wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay at an angle: cuneiform.

Was Nisa the first named woman in history?

No. The “Nisa” story is widely repeated online, but it lacks scholarly basis, and no evidence shows the name even belonged to a woman.
