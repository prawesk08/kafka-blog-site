The Nobel That Didn’t Know Ed Boyden

Monday, October 5, 2026.

As it does every year, the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm announced this year’s Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. This year’s prize went to optogenetics, a technique that uses light to switch the brain’s nerve cells (neurons) on or off at will.

In short

  • A Nobel Prize can be shared by at most three people, and it is not given to anyone who has died.
  • On a research paper, the first name usually belongs to the student or researcher who did the work, and the last to the head of the lab. The Nobel usually goes to the last name.
  • The 2026 medicine prize for optogenetics went to Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel, but not to Ed Boyden, first author of the 2005 paper that launched the field.
  • Shohei Hori, Rosalind Lee, Douglas Prasher, Jocelyn Bell and Albert Schatz were left out the same way.
  • In 1903, Pierre Curie’s letter put Marie Curie on the Nobel stage, a rare exception.

In his will, Alfred Nobel arranged for each prize to be chosen by a separate institution. Every year the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences picks the physics and chemistry prizes, the Karolinska Institutet picks medicine, the Swedish Academy picks literature, and the Norwegian Nobel Committee picks the peace prize. Some of the Nobel rules, though, were made later, and two of them are very strict. A prize can be shared by at most three people, and it cannot be given to someone who has died.

A prize can be shared by at most three people, and it cannot be given to someone who has died.

This time there were three medicine laureates: Karl Deisseroth of the United States, and Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel of Germany. No one disputes the contribution of Hegemann and Nagel, who discovered a protein in green algae that opens in response to light. The question is about the two people who put it to use in nerve cells: the third person the prize recognized, and the fourth who did not fit.

Within hours of the announcement, one question was going around social media: where is Ed Boyden? On the 2005 paper that laid the foundation of optogenetics, the first name was Boyden’s and the last was Deisseroth’s. That paper, which showed how the protein Nagel and Hegemann had discovered could be used in nerve cells, is what started the new field of optogenetics.

At the time, Boyden and Deisseroth were both at Stanford University. Boyden, who was doing his PhD in another lab, worked on optogenetics with Deisseroth as a side project. Deisseroth was just starting his independent career.

Today Deisseroth is a professor at Stanford, and Boyden is at MIT. The optogenetics paper that Boyden began as a side project won the Nobel. The Nobel committee recognized Deisseroth, but there was no fourth chair for Boyden.

Every year, during Nobel week, the story of Boyden and Deisseroth repeats itself. On every research paper, the first name belongs to the student or researcher who played the main role in the work, and the last to the senior researcher or head of the lab. Prizes and credit alike usually recognize the author whose name comes last.

This story is not only this year’s, and it is not new. The history of science is full of Ed Boydens. But there was one scientist who turned this custom upside down 123 years ago.

Marie Curie: The First Woman to Win a Nobel Prize

June 25, 1903, the Sorbonne, Paris.

A thirty-five-year-old Polish woman stood before her examiners to defend her PhD research. The subject of her thesis was “Research on Radioactive Substances”.

Exactly six years earlier, she had started her PhD research by picking up a small question. The French scientist Henri Becquerel had already shown that uranium gives off an invisible “new kind” of ray. But what the ray was, how strong it was, and whether other substances gave it off too, no one knew. She set out to follow it. She found that thorium, not only uranium, gave off the same kind of rays, and she named the phenomenon “radioactivity”.

When she began, it was already known that uranium was radioactive. But no one had noticed that pitchblende, the mineral uranium is mined from, contains another element far more radioactive than uranium. While measuring the radioactivity of pitchblende, that new element caught her attention, and finding out what it was became the heart of her PhD.

By this point her husband had set aside his own research to join her. Within 1898 the two of them found two new elements, polonium and radium. They spent the next four years in a leaky shed, boiling tons of pitchblende and stirring it with an iron rod as tall as themselves, to separate pure radium.

The researcher standing before the examiners on June 25, 1903 was Marie Curie, and sitting in the audience was her husband and colleague, Pierre Curie.

Early in 1903, members of the French Academy of Sciences had nominated two people for that year’s Nobel Prize in Physics: Henri Becquerel and Pierre Curie. The woman whose question and research had started it all did not even make the list.

That year the Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler sat on the Nobel committee. This bothered him, and he wrote to Pierre, telling him that only his name had been put forward for the prize. In August, Pierre wrote back: “If it is true that one is seriously thinking about me for the prize, I very much wish to be considered together with Madame Curie with respect to our research on radioactive bodies.” Many accounts say Pierre declared, “I won’t accept the prize without Marie.” His letter uses no such strong words. It was gentle, but its meaning was clear.

“If it is true that one is seriously thinking about me for the prize, I very much wish to be considered together with Madame Curie.” (Pierre Curie, 1903)

In December 1903, the physics prize was awarded for radioactivity: half to Becquerel and half to the Curies. Marie became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize. She is also the only person to win a Nobel for their PhD research. Eight years later, in 1911, she won the chemistry prize alone, for her research on radium and polonium.

Pierre had nothing to lose. His name was already known, the Academy had chosen him, and credit was flowing naturally his way. He simply did not want it to.

But turn the pages of the history of science and you find few people like Pierre. Look no further than the greatest scientist you and I know, Albert Einstein. Two years after Marie’s Nobel Prize, he published five research papers, including E=mc². We know 1905 as Einstein’s miracle year.

His only Nobel Prize (for 1921) was for the work on light he published early that year. His wife Mileva did the mathematics in all those papers. Yet Mileva’s name appears nowhere on any of Einstein’s papers.

The DNA Secret Hidden in a Drawer

Fifty years after Marie’s first Nobel Prize, Linus Pauling in America and James Watson, Francis Crick and Maurice Wilkins in Britain were racing to find the structure of DNA. The most important contribution of all came from Rosalind Franklin, but no one valued it at the time.

In early January 1953, Pauling put out a model of DNA, and on January 29 Watson, in Cambridge, got to read it. The next morning Watson went to London to tell Wilkins, “Pauling has proposed a DNA model that doesn’t work either, just like ours.” In Wilkins’s lab at King’s College, empty for the winter break, they spent the whole day discussing what to do next.

Rosalind’s lab was right next to Wilkins’s. Suddenly he remembered a photograph of DNA that Rosalind had taken about eight months earlier. Rosalind was not in the lab that day. He neither asked her permission nor told her, once she was back, that he had shown the photo to Watson. On January 30, 1953, Watson saw Photo 51, which Rosalind and her student Raymond Gosling had taken with an exposure of about 60 hours. It was Photo 51 that revealed the secret of how DNA is built.

Having seen Photo 51, Watson hurried back to Cambridge. On the cold train from London, he sketched the photo’s “X” from memory in the margin of a newspaper. The moment he saw the photo, it was clear to him: DNA is made of two strands in a helix, a double helix. Rosalind’s photo could tell you everything, the length of the helix and the distance between two nucleotides.

For Watson and Crick, Rosalind’s Photo 51 was the eureka moment. Watson later wrote, “The instant I saw the picture my mouth fell open and my pulse began to race.”

Two days after returning from London, they went back to work on DNA. Watson and Crick’s paper on the structure of DNA appeared in Nature on April 25, 1953. Rosalind and Gosling’s paper with Photo 51 was printed in the same issue. The credit for discovering the structure of DNA, and about a decade later the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins.

Rosalind, who took Photo 51, had already died in 1958, at the age of 37. She left King’s College in 1953 on the condition that she would not work on DNA again, and spent the rest of her life researching viruses. The Nobel Prize is still not given to scientists who have died. Even had she lived, she would hardly have fit among the three laureates.

Whose Work, Whose Name?

I have told Rosalind’s story many times. But every time I told it, I repeated the same mistake. With Rosalind at the center of the story, I rarely remembered her PhD student, Raymond Gosling, who worked right beside her. The two of them contributed equally to telling apart the “A” and “B” forms of DNA for the first time.

On May 2, 1952, Rosalind and Gosling took Photo 51 together, an exposure of nearly 60 hours. I remembered the injustice done to Rosalind, but it turns out that I, too, kept forgetting the student who sat beside the camera.

I remembered the injustice done to Rosalind, but it turns out that I, too, kept forgetting the student who sat beside the camera.

But the Rosalind story doesn’t end here. This is not the only time a Rosalind missed a Nobel Prize she deserved.

In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun for discovering a new kind of RNA called microRNA. The Nobel Prize’s official Twitter account wrote that Ambros celebrated the news with his colleague and wife, Rosalind Lee.

Rosalind was the lead author of the 1993 research paper on microRNA, and the last name on it was Ambros’s. The prize forgot Rosalind and recognized only Ambros.

The same thing happened last year, when the 2025 medicine prize was awarded. Mary Brunkow, Fred Ramsdell and Shimon Sakaguchi won the Nobel for their discoveries about the regulatory cells of our immune system, regulatory T cells (Tregs).

The researcher in Sakaguchi’s lab who showed that the Foxp3 gene controls how Tregs develop and work was Shohei Hori. On the 2003 paper on Tregs and Foxp3, the first name was his and the last was Sakaguchi’s. He did not fit in the prize’s three chairs; the prize recognized only the head of the lab.

Still, last year’s prize also hides a story that runs the other way. Mary Brunkow, who identified the Foxp3 gene in mice in 2001, did win the Nobel. She was not a university professor but a researcher working at a company. Nobel laureates from outside universities are rare.

I myself do research in a university lab in New York. In our world, the first name on a paper usually belongs to the student or postdoc who repeated the experiments for months and produced the data. The last name belongs to the head of the lab (the PI), who set the direction of the research and raised the money. They are also the ones who correspond with the journal when the paper is published. And when prizes are announced or history is written, it is usually their name the world remembers.

Back in 1968, the sociologist Robert Merton called this the “Matthew effect”: whoever is already known gets the credit too. Because it falls even harder on women, the historian Margaret Rossiter named that version the “Matilda effect” in 1993. Leaf through history and the stories of Boyden, Rosalind and Gosling may stop looking like exceptions.

The Professor Gets the Name, the Researcher Stays Nameless

There was a time when catching tuberculosis (TB) meant waiting to die. The first drug to lift that fear was streptomycin. In 1943, a professor at Rutgers University in the United States kept a 23-year-old student who worked with TB bacteria in the basement of the building, away from everyone else.

The professor never set foot in that lab himself. The student worked there day and night, sometimes sleeping there too. Within a few months, from a bacterium taken from a chicken’s throat, he isolated streptomycin, which could kill the TB bacterium.

The professor’s name was Selman Waksman, and the student was Albert Schatz. On their 1944 paper, the first name was the student’s, and both their names were on the patent. The professor had the student sign his royalties over to the university for one dollar, while he himself quietly kept taking 20 percent of the royalties. When the student found out, he sued in 1950, and in an out-of-court settlement he was recognized as “co-discoverer”.

After he sued his own professor, American universities hesitated to hire him. Two years later, in 1952, Waksman alone received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of streptomycin. Rutgers finally recognized Schatz nearly 50 years later, honoring him in 1994.

Jocelyn Bell and the Signal from Dead Stars

Physics has a similar example of a hardworking student who got no credit. By 1967, on a field outside Cambridge, a radio telescope had been built over two years by stringing thousands of wires across hundreds of posts.

Every day the telescope recorded the signals coming from the sky as lines traced on long rolls of paper. The job of reading those lines and working out what the signals were fell to 24-year-old Jocelyn Bell.

One day in August she noticed a rough, scruffy-looking mark on the paper, about a centimeter long. Someone else might have dismissed it as a glitch in the machine. She remembered it, waited for the same patch of sky again, and found it again. Recorded up close, the signal turned out to be pulsing like a clock, exactly 1.33 seconds apart. No one could believe anything in nature was that regular, so as a joke it was named “LGM-1”, for Little Green Men. Later it turned out to be a dead star spinning extremely fast: a pulsar.

On the paper published in Nature in 1968, the first name was her supervisor Antony Hewish’s, and hers was second. In 1974 the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Hewish and Martin Ryle. Jocelyn herself never complained; she said that giving a Nobel to a student would be right only in exceptional cases.

Jocelyn’s contribution was recognized only later, and she used the prize she received in the same spirit. In 2018 she gave away the whole of the roughly three million dollars of her Breakthrough Prize to students from underrepresented communities who want to study physics.

Lise Meitner and the Atom Worked Out in the Snow

Another physicist who did not complain was Lise Meitner. Before the Second World War, she and her colleague Otto Hahn were already working on what would become nuclear fission. Lise was a woman, and she was Jewish. After nearly thirty years working with Hahn in Berlin, she fled Nazi Germany in July 1938 to save her life and reached Sweden.

Even after she reached Sweden, her work with Hahn went on by letter. In Berlin, when Hahn and his assistant Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons, they found barium, another element about half as heavy as uranium. In December 1938, Hahn wrote to Lise asking her to explain it.

That Christmas holiday, walking in the Swedish snow with her nephew, the physicist Otto Frisch, Lise sat down on a tree trunk and did the calculation. The uranium nucleus had split in two, and the energy released could be measured with Einstein’s own E=mc². Frisch named it “fission”. But Lise’s name was not on Hahn and Strassmann’s paper, published in January 1939. Putting her name on it would have told the Nazis that she was still working with them.

Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry; even Strassmann’s name did not come up. Lise was nominated for the Nobel nearly fifty times, but she never won. Why she was left out we learned only much later. When the Nobel files, kept secret for 50 years, were opened in the 1990s, historians wrote that the committee had simply failed to understand Lise’s contribution, which sat on the border between physics and chemistry.

Lise’s name is not on the list of Nobel laureates, but it is on the list of elements: element number 109, meitnerium.

The Glowing Green Gene Sent by Mail

For people like me who work on the immune system, the protein that makes cells glow green (GFP) is an everyday tool. It comes from a species of jellyfish, and the first person to isolate its gene, in 1992, was Douglas Prasher. Right then his research funding ran out. Before leaving science, he mailed the gene, free of charge, to two scientists: Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.

In 2008, when Osamu Shimomura, who discovered the protein, and Chalfie and Tsien, who researched the gene, won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, Prasher was driving a shuttle van for a car dealership in Alabama. Chalfie and Tsien, though, did not forget him: they invited him to Stockholm at their own expense. When the prize was handed over, Prasher was there too, not on the stage, but in the audience.

The List Doesn’t End Here

Start counting such names and the list never ends. But a few more must be remembered.

In 1921, the team in Canada that discovered insulin included a 22-year-old student, Charles Best. But two years later the Nobel went to Frederick Banting and the head of the lab, John Macleod. Furious that the prize went to Macleod instead of Best, Banting left the lab and gave half of his prize to Best.

Another example is Candace Pert: although she discovered the opiate receptor in the brain during her PhD research, the prize went to three men, including her professor.

Like history itself, science has been most unjust to women. It was Nettie Stevens who showed that the X and Y chromosomes decide whether an animal is male or female, yet for decades textbooks put Edmund B. Wilson’s name first. Marthe Gautier was the first to see the cause of Down syndrome under the microscope, but the world recognized her colleague Jérôme Lejeune.

Some die before any prize can reach them. Alice Ball, who made the first effective injectable treatment for leprosy, died at just 24, and her method went around under the name of her college’s president, as the “Dean method”. Henrietta Leavitt, who found the rule for measuring distances across the universe, had also died before anyone tried to nominate her for the Nobel.

And in our own neighborhood: Satyendra Nath Bose, who sent his mathematics from Dhaka to Einstein, gave his name to a whole family of particles, the “bosons”. Many people later won Nobel Prizes for discoveries about bosons. Bose himself never did.

One Stage, Three Chairs

The Nobel has its own arithmetic: one stage, at most three chairs, and the condition that you must be alive.

The fourth chair: people who did the work but were left off the Nobel stage (created with Claude).

The three-chair rule was made in an era when science was done by one or two people working together. Today a single research paper carries dozens, sometimes hundreds of names, but there are still only three chairs on the Nobel stage. This year there was no fourth chair for Ed Boyden, and in 2008 there was none for Douglas Prasher.

The must-be-alive condition kept Rosalind Franklin, Lise and Henrietta Leavitt out forever. But just how strange this condition is became clear in 2011. That year, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was announced for Ralph Steinman, who discovered the dendritic cells of our immune system. The committee had no idea, but he had died three days earlier. The Nobel Foundation let the prize stand in his name. In other words, who gets a Nobel is sometimes decided not by their contribution but by when the news of a death reaches Stockholm.

But the biggest gap is not in any written rule; it is in science’s unwritten custom. Albert Schatz, Candace Pert, Rosalind Lee, Shohei Hori and Ed Boyden were all the first name on the papers reporting their discoveries. The prize went to the last name, the corresponding author. The corresponding author is the person who corresponds with the journal’s editor and takes responsibility for the paper; it is not proof of who made the discovery. But over time, grants, prizes and history all came to treat that address as the discoverer.

The biggest gap is not in any written rule; it is in science’s unwritten custom.

Not every lab head is a credit thief. Sakaguchi and Deisseroth built their fields through decades of hard work. But the system has become such that no one needs to steal the credit; it flows on its own, looking for whoever sits on top.

A century and a quarter ago, one letter from Pierre Curie refused this custom. Since him, few have written such a letter. In 1923 Banting split his prize money with Best, and in 2008 Chalfie and Tsien brought Prasher to Stockholm. But those remained exceptions.

Early next October, the names of the new Nobel laureates will once again be announced from Stockholm. That day, after you read the winners’ names, open their research papers and look at whose name comes first. Perhaps you will find a Boyden there, a Hori, a Rosalind.

The Nobel doesn’t read their names. Let us, at least.

Frequently asked questions

How many people can share a Nobel Prize?

At most three. The prize also cannot be given to someone who has died, though a laureate who dies after the announcement keeps it. In 2011 the Nobel Foundation let Ralph Steinman’s prize stand, even though he had died three days before the announcement.

Why didn’t Ed Boyden share the 2026 Nobel Prize?

The Nobel committee does not explain its choices. The prize went to Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel; with only three places, there was no room for Boyden, first author of the 2005 paper that launched optogenetics.

What is the Matthew effect in science?

A term coined by the sociologist Robert Merton in 1968: credit tends to flow to scientists who are already well known. Because it falls even harder on women, the historian Margaret Rossiter called that version the Matilda effect in 1993.

Did Marie Curie almost miss her first Nobel Prize?

Yes. The 1903 nomination named only Henri Becquerel and Pierre Curie. After Gösta Mittag-Leffler alerted Pierre, he wrote asking to be considered together with Marie, and she shared the prize.

Who took Photo 51?

Rosalind Franklin and her PhD student Raymond Gosling, in May 1952, at King’s College London. Maurice Wilkins showed it to James Watson in January 1953 without Franklin’s knowledge.
