This article was first published in Nepali in the “Basava: Excavating Time” column on Shilapatra on September 30, 2026 (Ashwin 14, 2083 BS). This is the author’s own English translation.

The article’s header as published on Shilapatra.

Because the Swami-king had no dharma, the children were driven out; we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
Shri Rana Bahadur, sovereign of Nepal: the people suffered greatly.
Do not, do not, Shitala Maju, we beg you a thousand times: deliver this world.

This is how the hymn “Shitala Maju” ends. In this two-hundred-year-old Newar folk song there is the name of a king, and a prayer to the goddess of smallpox. The hymn was born in Rana Bahadur’s palace in Kathmandu. Those who wrote it and sang it were the people driven out of Kathmandu by the king’s decree. The hymn of Shitala Maju is the story of the people chased out of the Valley because of smallpox.

The Smallpox That Entered the Palace

By early 1799, a smallpox epidemic had already spread through Kathmandu. Smallpox was nothing new for the Kathmandu Valley. It came every few years, took some children, and the survivors grew up with scars across their faces. But that year smallpox did not stop at the city’s alleys; it entered the palace itself. It reached even Kantivati, King Rana Bahadur Shah’s most beloved queen. (Tom Robertson, “Smallpox, politics and power,” 2020)

In short

  • In 1799 a smallpox epidemic in Kathmandu reached Kantivati, the favourite queen of King Rana Bahadur Shah.
  • After she died, Rana Bahadur had the temple of Hariti (Shitala Maju) at Swayambhu torn down.
  • To protect his son Girvan, he ordered children with smallpox and their families driven across the Tamakoshi and the Marsyangdi.
  • The Newar hymn “Shitala Maju” preserves the journey of those families, night by night.
  • Seventeen years later, in 1816, Girvan himself died of smallpox.

Rana Bahadur Shah, the grandson born in 1775, a few months after Prithvi Narayan Shah died, became king as a small child. He was made king on paper at just two and a half, but at first his mother, Rajendra Laxmi, ruled as regent. She died in 1785, and from then until 1794 his uncle Bahadur Shah held the regency. It was Bahadur Shah who, around early 1790, sent the teenage Rana Bahadur to Janakpur and the Tarai to learn about his country.

Born into a Brahmin family in Mahottari, Kantivati Devi Jha was famous across Mithila for her beauty and her voice. As was the custom of the time, she was married young, but her husband, Jagannath Jha, died within a few years. The widowed Kantivati was living in her parents’ home. Rana Bahadur met her there when he reached Janakpur.

Smitten at their first meeting, Rana Bahadur proposed marriage on the spot. In a society where a widow was not allowed to remarry at all, such a proposal from the king stunned the priests and courtiers of the palace. Kantivati herself, afraid of sin, refused for a long time. She kept saying, “You are the king; understand dharma too. I am a widow, you are the king. Our relationship will destroy dharma, society and the kingdom alike.” But Rana Bahadur listened to no one. He brought Kantivati with him to Kathmandu. Some accounts also say that Kantivati came to Kathmandu later, in 1794, with her own family to visit Pashupati, and met Rana Bahadur again there.

In 1794, Bahadur Shah’s regency was taken from him. Rana Bahadur was no longer a king only on paper. Kantivati, too, agreed to marry, on one condition: her son must be made king. Rana Bahadur accepted her condition at once. In January 1797 Kantivati entered the palace as Rana Bahadur’s third queen and was given the rank of Rajeshwari Rani. On October 19 of that same year their son, Girvan Yuddha, was born.

For Kantivati, Rana Bahadur was ready to go to any length. Because his uncle Bahadur Shah had opposed the marriage, he had him imprisoned. Bahadur Shah, who had unified half of Nepal, was put under house arrest at Pashupati. In June 1797 he was brutally killed, with boiling oil poured over his body, on the orders of his own nephew. (Yadav Devkota, Setopati, citing sources from Bahadur Shah’s family) Keeping his promise to the queen, Rana Bahadur declared Girvan crown prince within days. Rana Bahadur’s eldest son, Ranodyot, was removed as crown prince and, in November 1797, exiled to Nuwakot. His descendants are still with us. Nabin Prakash Jung Shah, a former vice-chancellor of Tribhuvan University, is a descendant of Ranodyot. His father, Jagat Jung Shah, was killed in Chitwan in 1963 on King Mahendra’s orders; Jagat Jung was a great-grandson of Ranodyot, Rana Bahadur’s eldest son.

A few months after the infant Girvan was declared crown prince, smallpox began to appear in Kathmandu. By early 1799 it had become an epidemic, and it reached Kantivati too. More than the smallpox, the queen was consumed by worry about what would happen to her infant son after her death. On March 8, 1799, Rana Bahadur abdicated the throne himself and declared Girvan, not yet eighteen months old, king. He proclaimed himself Swami Nirgunananda, left the palace, and settled in Deopatan to nurse his sick queen. This is where the rise of Bhimsen Thapa begins. (Robertson, 2020)

Kantivati died in Deopatan on October 31, 1799. Opinions differ on whether her death was caused by smallpox or was a suicide. According to Henry Oldfield, the British Residency surgeon who came to Nepal about sixty years after these events, the queen had in fact recovered from smallpox, but took her own life because she could not bear to live with a face the disease had disfigured. (Henry Oldfield, Sketches from Nipal, 1880)

After losing his beloved queen, Rana Bahadur’s grief turned to rage. Swami Nirgunananda had now become a deranged king. His fury fell hardest on the goddess of smallpox. He had the temple of Hariti, that is Shitala Maju, at Swayambhu torn down and banned her worship. Before the Hariti temple was demolished, he had the image “worshipped” with excrement. He is also said to have marched on Pashupati with a cannon to destroy it. In a country that Pashupati had always protected, that day someone had to protect Pashupati from the king’s wrath. (Oldfield, 1880; Susan Heydon, “Death of the King,” 2019; Rajendra Maharjan, Sitala Maju Mye, 2022)

Then Rana Bahadur began to fear for his child king. To protect his son from smallpox, he issued a decree: every child in the Valley who had smallpox must leave the Valley with their whole family, crossing the Tamakoshi to the east or the Marsyangdi to the west. Because the Valley was home mostly to the Newar community, most of the families driven out were Newar. (Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020)

The Journey Remembered in the Shitala Maju Hymn

Those driven from the Valley never got to have their suffering written into any record, but that suffering lived on as a song. Shitala Maju, sung by an unknown traveller driven out of Kathmandu in the early nineteenth century, is still one of the best-known hymns in Newar society. (Prem Bahadur Kansakar, Bakham Mye, 1973; Siegfried Lienhard, Songs of Nepal, 1992; Maharjan, 2022)

Those driven from the Valley never got to have their suffering written into any record, but that suffering lived on as a song.

The hymn begins with the king’s decree. This is how it remembers the start of that day:

O Shitala Maju, look at the state of your people!
Never heard of, never seen: no small child may stay in the land, so the Maharaja has decreed.
With the naykhin drum beating, with soldiers closing in, they drove the little children out.
Beaten rice tucked under the arm, children on our backs, we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
One child on the back, one on the hip, another dragged along by the hand.

In its very first line the hymn calls on Shitala Maju, and in the second it names the source of the suffering: the Maharaja’s decree. The Valley had never before lived through such an order, “never heard of, never seen.” Smallpox had come every few years before this too, but no king had ever driven sick children, along with their families, out of the country.

The decree was announced with drums, and soldiers formed a cordon and drove the families out of their neighbourhoods. The only thing they were allowed to carry for the road was beaten rice. And the hymn even counts how many children one mother or father could carry: one on the back, one on the hip, and a third, old enough to walk, dragged along by the hand.

The journey that began with the naykhin drum and the soldiers’ cordon is preserved in the hymn night by night, turn by turn. From Kathmandu through Bhaktapur, Banepa, Palanchok and Dolalghat to the Tamakoshi: five nights’ stops. On the first night they stayed in Bhaktapur and prayed to Taleju. On the second night, in Banepa, they prayed to Chandeshwari; then to Bhagawati in Palanchok, and to Bhimsen in Dolalghat. At every turn of the journey there was a god, but no god showed them the way home.

This part of the hymn runs like a map:

Leaving Kathmandu, we spent the night in Bhaktapur and prayed to Taleju Maju.
Leaving Bhaktapur, we spent the night in Banepa and prayed to Chandeshwari.
Leaving Banepa, we spent the night in Palanchok and prayed to Bhagawati.
Leaving Palanchok, we spent the night in Dolalghat and prayed to Bhimsen.
Leaving Dolalghat, we spent the night by the Tamakoshi; we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
Leaving Dolakha, we reached the far bank of the Tamakoshi and prayed to Mahadev.

The hymn has not forgotten what they endured on the road, either. There was no food to eat, no clothes to wear, no shelter to sleep in. The soldiers drove them forward not with whips or sticks but by beating them with stinging nettles. What nettles felt like on a child’s skin already blistered by smallpox, the hymn does not say.

No food to eat, no clothes to wear, no shelter to call my own.
Not with whips, not with sticks, but with nettles they struck us; the soldiers closed in and drove us on.

And the hymn keeps one more thing in memory. All along the road, the parents call on three goddesses: Kachala Maju, who raises the blisters on the skin; Shitala Maju, who fills those blisters with water; and Bachala Maju, who takes the sick away.

We pray to Kachala Maju who raises the blisters, Shitala Maju who fills them with water, and Bachala Maju who takes the sick away.
If this child lives, we will set free a pair of pigeons and offer jasmine flowers of gold and silver.

A society that had never heard the word virus remembered every stage of its most terrifying disease by giving it the name of a goddess. Open any modern textbook of medicine and you will find the course of smallpox written in the same order. First, raised spots (papules) appear on the skin; then they turn into fluid-filled blisters (vesicles); and then they fill with pus (pustules) and ripen. (Frank Fenner and others, Smallpox and its Eradication, 1988)

The second line holds a vow: if the child lives, they will release a pair of pigeons and offer jasmine flowers of gold and silver. Before a disease with no cure, a vow was the only remedy parents had left.

But the vow was not fulfilled. Of all those smallpox caught, Bachala Maju took nearly a third. The most heartbreaking part comes next. The hymn tells the story of a mother on the bank of the Tamakoshi like this:

In a place the sun never reaches, the child died of cold; mother and father beat their chests and wept.
The dead child may not be burned, may not be buried: look at the state of your people!
Mother holding half the body, father holding the other half, they gave the child to the Tamakoshi.

The child died not of smallpox but of cold. For a child weakened by smallpox, hungry and walking through the cold, a sunless gorge was enough. There was no cremation, not even a burial. Mother and father, each holding half the body, gave their child to the river.

Mother and father, each holding half the body, gave their child to the river.

In the end, the hymn does not hide whose fault this was:

Because the Swami-king had no dharma, the children were driven out; we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
Shri Rana Bahadur, sovereign of Nepal: the people suffered greatly.

When he gave the decree, Rana Bahadur had already left the throne and become Swami Nirgunananda. The hymn calls him both swami and king, and finds that neither had dharma. To cross the Tamakoshi was to leave the “Nepal” of that time.

Francis Buchanan-Hamilton, who came to Nepal at the start of the nineteenth century, wrote in 1819 that the eastern edge of Nepal Mandala was marked by the Bhimsen temple at Dolakha. (Francis Buchanan-Hamilton, An Account of the Kingdom of Nepal, 1819) Tom Robertson, who has written the history of smallpox in Nepal, also reads the Shitala Maju hymn as a symbol of what the Newars suffered after the Shahs took the Valley in the 1770s.

Some of those driven across the Tamakoshi began new lives around Dolakha and built a temple to Kanti Bhairav there. The Shitala Maju hymn found its home there. Many of those who reached the far side of the Tamakoshi never returned to Kathmandu. The roots of some Newar settlements outside the Valley lie in this forced displacement. (Mary Slusser, Nepal Mandala, 1982) History remembered Rana Bahadur’s decree as a tale of royal might, but the story of those driven out survived only in this hymn. (Mahesh Chandra Regmi, A Study in Nepali Economic History 1768–1846, 1971)

The goddess the parents pray to in the hymn was the very one whose temple the king had torn down a few months earlier. The king’s rage demolished the goddess’s temple, but it could not demolish the people’s faith. All along the road to the Tamakoshi, the parents who had been driven out called on her, and for two hundred years the Valley has sung the Shitala Maju hymn every winter.

The king’s rage demolished the goddess’s temple, but it could not demolish the people’s faith.

And Rana Bahadur’s decree did protect the boy king from smallpox. The children with smallpox were removed from the Valley, and Girvan never had to go through it. But smallpox has its own rule: those who caught it once and survived never caught it again, while those who never had it stayed at risk for the rest of their lives.

Seventeen years after Rana Bahadur’s decree, smallpox returned to the Valley, and once again it entered the palace. On November 21, 1816, Girvan, the son of Rana Bahadur and Kantivati, died of smallpox.

During the epidemic in which Girvan died, the smallpox vaccine had already reached Nepal. The vaccine came as far as the king’s door, but it could not reach the king. It took another hundred and fifty years for the people to get the vaccine that had reached the king’s door.

The vaccine came as far as the king’s door, but it could not reach the king.

Who is Shitala Maju? Why is the goddess of smallpox worshipped in winter and spring? And what happened to Girvan, the son his father had shielded from smallpox, and how was smallpox eradicated from Nepal? I will write about that in the next part.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Shitala Maju hymn?

A Newar devotional song about two hundred years old. It tells of the families driven out of the Kathmandu Valley by Rana Bahadur Shah’s decree and their journey to the Tamakoshi. It is still sung in winter.

Why did Rana Bahadur Shah expel children with smallpox?

To keep his infant son, King Girvan Yuddha, from catching smallpox. In 1799 he ordered sick children and their families out of the Valley, east across the Tamakoshi and west across the Marsyangdi.

Who was Kantivati?

Kantivati Devi Jha, a Brahmin widow from Mahottari, became Rana Bahadur’s third queen and the mother of King Girvan Yuddha. She died in Deopatan on October 31, 1799.

How did King Girvan Yuddha die?

Of smallpox, in November 1816. The smallpox vaccine had already reached Nepal by then, but he was never vaccinated.
