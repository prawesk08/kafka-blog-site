The article’s header as published on Shilapatra.

In Part I

The smallpox that spread through Kathmandu in early 1799 reached even Kantivati, King Rana Bahadur Shah’s most beloved queen. Rana Bahadur declared his son Girvan Yuddha, not yet eighteen months old, king. He became Swami Nirgunananda and devoted himself to nursing the queen in Deopatan. But on October 31, Kantivati died.

After her death, Rana Bahadur, driven mad by grief, turned his rage on the goddess of smallpox. He had the Shitala/Hariti Maju temple at Swayambhu torn down and ordered that children with smallpox be driven out of the Valley with their families. The suffering of those families, who reached the Tamakoshi surrounded by soldiers and lashed with nettles, is still sung every winter as the hymn “Shitala Maju.”

That year, Rana Bahadur’s decree did protect the boy Girvan from smallpox. But smallpox has its own rules. Seventeen years after the families were expelled, smallpox returned to the Valley. And this time it took Girvan Yuddha himself, the son of Rana Bahadur and Kantivati.

But before Girvan’s story, we need to understand who “Shitala Maju” is: the goddess the parents called on all along the road, and whose temple the king tore down.

From Child-Eating Demoness to Protector of Children: The Strange Origin of Hariti/Shitala Maju

In short

  • Hariti, or Shitala Maju, is a goddess worshipped by Buddhists and Hindus alike: once a child-eating demoness, she became the protector of children.
  • Smallpox peaked in the Valley in winter and spring, when the virus survives longest in cold, dry air and when traders from India and Tibet arrived.
  • Vaccine reached Kathmandu in 1816, but while the palace waited, King Girvan Yuddha died of smallpox.
  • The vaccine never left the palace for the people; a national programme began only in the 1960s.
  • Nepal’s last smallpox case was found in 1975, and the country was declared smallpox-free in 1977.

The temple Rana Bahadur had torn down stood at Swayambhu, and the goddess worshipped there has many names. For Buddhists she is Hariti, for Hindus Shitala Mata, and for Newars Ajima or Maju. Worshipped in a two-storey temple to the north-west of the Swayambhu chaitya, she is regarded as the protector of children under twelve and the guardian of the Swayambhu area. (Min Bahadur Shakya, “Hariti Temple of Swayambhu,” 1996)

Her beginnings, though, were the opposite. In the Buddhist story, Hariti was a yakshini who stole and ate other people’s children to feed her own hundreds of offspring. The Buddha hid her most beloved daughter, Ananda, in his begging bowl. (In some versions it is her youngest son rather than a daughter.) When Hariti, exhausted from searching the mountains and the seas for her daughter, came to him, the Buddha asked: if losing one child hurts this much, how much must it hurt those whose children you ate? From then on Hariti vowed never again to harm a child, and instead to protect all children. (Shakya, 1996; Noël Péri, “Hārītī, la Mère-de-démons,” 1917)

Hariti’s story and worship are believed to have begun in Gandhara (today’s Pakistan and Afghanistan), and they travelled with Buddhism through Central Asia to China and Japan. In Japan she is still worshipped as Kishimojin. In the Kathmandu Valley, she became a goddess of both Buddhists and Hindus.

Even today, devotees of both religions come to the Hariti temple at Swayambhu to make offerings and vows, asking her to protect their children from disease and epidemics. (Péri, 1917; Swayambhu Stupa, “Harati Ajimaa Temple”)

It is no coincidence that the goddess of the disease that struck children hardest is also the protector of children. One and the same goddess can take a child away and can also save it. For a society with no medicine against smallpox, this was the greatest hope of all: do not let the goddess grow angry, and she will give the child back.

One and the same goddess can take a child away and can also save it.

The word “Shitala” itself means “the one who cools”: the goddess who cools the high fever of smallpox and the burning of its blisters. In northern India she is shown riding a donkey, holding a broom and a pot of cold water. The custom on Shitala Ashtami of not lighting the stove and offering food cooked the day before is a symbol of that same coolness. (Fabrizio Ferrari, Religion, Devotion and Medicine in North India, 2015)

In many parts of South Asia, smallpox was seen not just as a disease but as the arrival of the goddess. A house with smallpox was a house the goddess had come to. (Ralph Nicholas, “The Goddess Śītalā and Epidemic Smallpox in Bengal,” 1981)

This is part of what made Rana Bahadur’s rage so strange. The very goddess the people worshipped as the protector of their children, the king saw as the enemy who had taken his child and his queen. The temple he tore down did not stay in ruins for long. After Rana Bahadur gave up the throne, the temple was purified and reopened, and the goddess’s worship resumed. (Heydon, 2019)

But both the worship of Shitala Maju and the arrival of smallpox follow a calendar of their own.

Why Did Smallpox Return in Winter?

Even today, the Shitala Maju hymn is one of the seasonal songs of the Newar community, and it is sung in winter, around Poush and Magh. Shitala Maju’s annual festival, Shitala Ashtami, also falls in Chaitra, after Holi. Every spring, communities still pray to be protected from smallpox. This is not just a coincidence.

Old accounts of Nepal show smallpox spreading most in the Valley from the end of winter through spring, with cases falling during the monsoon from June to September. Every spring, communities prayed for protection from smallpox. The smallpox virus (variola) survives a long time in cold, dry air and is destroyed quickly in warm, humid air. (Fenner and others, 1988; Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020b)

Half the answer to why smallpox kept returning to Kathmandu every few years lies in the Valley’s weather. The rest lies hidden in the roads that led to the Valley.

Daniel Wright, the British Residency surgeon who came to Rana-era Nepal about seventy years after Rana Bahadur’s decree, wrote about Hetauda. Hetauda, the gateway on the road from India to Kathmandu, was deserted from April to November, because its malaria killed everyone except the Tharus. So almost all goods and people entering Nepal crossed the Tarai in winter. A checkpoint was kept there in winter, but even its soldiers climbed into the hills as soon as the hot season began. (Daniel Wright, History of Nepal, 1877, p. 3; Heydon, 2019)

The story of the northern route was much the same. Henry Oldfield, another Residency surgeon who came to Nepal a few years before Wright, noted that Tibetan traders left the cold plateau and came down into warmer Nepal from November to March. (Henry Oldfield, Sketches from Nipal, 1880, vol. 1, p. 11) Indians from the south and Tibetans from the north both filled the Valley in winter. And exactly then, smallpox spread through the Valley. Smallpox was a disease of winter and spring, and it was seen less in the heat and monsoon from June to September. (P.N. Shrestha, Smallpox Eradication in Nepal, 1978; Heydon, 2019)

Smallpox in Nepal’s History: Not Even Kings Were Spared

The history of smallpox in Nepal is not limited to the Shitala Maju hymn. Smallpox did not distinguish rich from poor, or king from subject.

Records of smallpox taking a king in Nepal go back to the Malla period: in 1715, King Mahindra Malla of Lalitpur died of smallpox. (Baburam Acharya, “King Prithvi Narayan Shah,” Regmi Research Series 8(8), 1976, p. 154) If even people inside the palace could not escape smallpox, it is not hard to imagine what it was like in the towns and villages. But no one kept count of the subjects who died; only the names of kings survived in the records. (Heydon, 2019)

Nearly sixty years after Mahindra Malla died, the shadow of smallpox fell on the Gorkhali palace too. Pratap Singh Shah, Prithvi Narayan Shah’s eldest son and successor, died at just twenty-six. When he died on November 17, 1777, he had reigned for only two years and ten months. Donald Hopkins, who wrote a world history of smallpox, says Pratap Singh died of smallpox. Others, however, believe the cause was tuberculosis. (Donald Hopkins, The Greatest Killer, 2002, p. 152)

Whatever the cause of Pratap Singh’s death, it put the two-and-a-half-year-old Rana Bahadur on the throne. The man whose father’s death is linked to smallpox would, twenty-two years later, tear down the temple of the goddess of smallpox.

By Rana Bahadur’s reign, smallpox was already a recognised disease with its own name among Nepalis. William Kirkpatrick, the British envoy who came to Nepal in 1793, compiled word lists of the Parbate and Newar languages. They contain only general words describing symptoms for other diseases.

But for smallpox, both languages have a name of its own: “bephen” in Parbate and “tau rye/tau nye” in Newar. (William Kirkpatrick, An Account of the Kingdom of Nepaul, 1811, p. 238; Maharjan, 2022) Kirkpatrick also described Kathmandu’s alleys as narrow and filthy, like those of Benares. In such a crowded, haphazard settlement, it was no surprise that smallpox spread again and again. (Kirkpatrick, p. 160; P.N. Shrestha, “History of Smallpox,” 1972; Heydon, 2019)

Nepal already had a home remedy for fighting smallpox. Taking pus from a smallpox patient’s blister and introducing it into a healthy person’s skin to cause a mild illness, and so give lifelong protection, is called variolation. The method was in use in South Asia before the Europeans arrived. It is thought to have been known in Nepal as early as Prithvi Narayan Shah’s time. (Shrestha, 1972; Heydon, 2019) Oldfield, in his list of Newar castes, identifies a group called “Kausa” as professional smallpox inoculators. (Oldfield, 1880, vol. 1, p. 186; Maharjan, 2022)

Smallpox also turns up here and there in government records. A record from 1862 BS (1805 CE) mentions smallpox spreading among jhara labourers at Chisapani (Makwanpur). (Regmi Research Series 20(11), 1988, p. 160) Jhara was compulsory unpaid labour the state took from its subjects. Villagers were sent in turns to build forts, roads, bridges and postal stations. Chisapani Garhi, on the postal route to and from Kathmandu, was a hub of communication where people from different parts of the country gathered for jhara, and they could carry disease home with them when they returned. (Mahesh Chandra Regmi, A Study in Nepali Economic History, 1971; Heydon, 2019)

Nepal’s very first census, carried out under Jung Bahadur in 1856, recorded 2,000 houses in Patan and 500 in Bhaktapur emptied by a smallpox epidemic. (Regmi Research Series 2(5), 1970, pp. 117–18) A few decades later, in 1885, Hari Ram, an Indian explorer secretly surveying the Everest region, wrote that smallpox coming from the east had taken many of the people of Khumbu, and that the route towards the Arun Kosi had closed and disappeared. (Records of the Survey of India, vol. 8, 1915, p. 386; Heydon, 2019)

But the most detailed account in all these records is from 1816, the year smallpox took Girvan Yuddha, that same son of Rana Bahadur and Kantivati.

The Vaccine Came as Far as the Palace, but Not to the King

When Girvan Yuddha was born, a rumour went around the palace: “Brahmin astrologers have prophesied that this boy, born of the ‘improper’ union of a Brahmin widow and a Kshatriya king, will die of smallpox.” Nineteen years later, the time came for that rumour to come true. (Chittaranjan Nepali, “Reign and Abdication of King Rana Bahadur Shah,” 1971; Heydon, 2019)

After losing the Anglo-Nepal War of 1814–16, Nepal had to accept a permanent British Resident in Kathmandu under the Treaty of Sugauli. When the first Resident, Edward Gardner, arrived in mid-1816, a smallpox epidemic was raging in the Kathmandu Valley. In a letter to Calcutta in early July, he says the epidemic, which began in western Nepal, had reached Kathmandu. According to the Indian historian Vikramjit Hasrat, who wrote about Nepal, Kathmandu’s rivers, ponds and canals at that time were “full of the dead and dying, there was no room to walk in the streets, and dogs could be seen everywhere dragging unclaimed corpses.” (Vikramjit Hasrat, History of Nepal, 1970; Robertson, 2020c)

The palace had only one worry: King Girvan had never had smallpox as a child. His junior queen, Goraksha Rajya Laxmi, had had smallpox in childhood and was considered safe, but the king was not. The very decree of Rana Bahadur in 1799 that drove children with smallpox out of the Valley was the reason Girvan never caught smallpox as a child.

By mid-July, the courtiers had gone to Resident Gardner to beg him to bring vaccine from India to protect the king from smallpox. In a letter to Calcutta on July 17, Gardner wrote that smallpox was raging in the country and that the Nepali courtiers were worried because the king had not yet had it. (Heydon, 2019)

Vaccination was a new technology at the time. The English physician Edward Jenner had published his results, showing that inoculating people with cowpox, a mild disease of cattle, could protect them from smallpox, only recently, in 1798. Variolation used human smallpox, which could sometimes kill the person inoculated and spread smallpox to others. Jenner’s vaccine carried no such risk. In India, the East India Company had been favouring the new vaccine over old-style variolation since 1804. (Fenner and others, Smallpox and its Eradication, 1988; Heydon, 2019)

Gardner saw a political opportunity in the courtiers’ request. In his letter to Calcutta of July 17 he wrote, “Introducing the vaccine is not only a humane act; having the king and courtiers vaccinated at English hands will also be diplomatically advantageous.” The war had only just ended, and the English needed Kathmandu’s trust. (Heydon, 2019)

Charles Everest, the Residency’s assistant surgeon, was given the job of obtaining vaccine material from Patna. Gardner himself also wrote private letters asking for vaccine to be sent from Calcutta. Letters alone took weeks to come and go, and in the monsoon the road through the Tarai was even harder. At last, at the end of August, the boxes of vaccine arrived, and on September 13 the vaccination campaign began in Banepa.

In his letters, Everest complains that the crowds coming to be vaccinated left him no free time at all. The decision to start the campaign in Banepa was Bhimsen Thapa’s; why, the answer is lost to history.

Courtiers and members of the leading families were vaccinated, but the king and his immediate family kept putting it off. At the end of October, Bhimsen Thapa himself went to the Resident with a proposal to vaccinate the king and the royal family immediately. But they decided to wait, saying vaccine that had come so far might not be reliable. The plan was to vaccinate a few children first, and then take fresh vaccine from their arms to give to the king.

For the king, that wait proved costly. It is not clear why they waited. Gardner had advised vaccinating at once, without waiting. Towards the end of October both of the king’s sons came down with fever. On November 2, his younger son Narendra, not yet two, died of smallpox, and his elder son Rajendra was ill too. (Heydon, 2019)

On November 18, 1816, the royal preceptor Gajaraj Mishra told the Resident that the king himself had smallpox. Two days later, on the night of November 20, the king’s condition worsened. As custom required, he was taken to Pashupati at dawn, and at about eleven o’clock the nineteen-year-old King Girvan Yuddha died there. His senior queen, Siddhi Rajya Laxmi, committed sati on his pyre. (Ludwig Stiller, The Silent Cry, 1976, p. 252)

Some time later, on December 16, the junior queen Goraksha Rajya Laxmi also died. Her death, too, was put down to smallpox. But Goraksha Rajya Laxmi had already had smallpox as a child. According to Gardner, she died in a premature childbirth brought on by distress. (Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020c)

Gardner wrote with regret that many chances to vaccinate the king had been lost “inexplicably.” Why they were lost is lost to history. The vaccine had come as far as the palace door, but it could not reach the king. The chronicles did not blame Bhimsen for the king’s death; they blamed Shitala Maju’s wrath. (Wright, 1877) The son’s death was put down to the anger of the very goddess whose temple his father had torn down.

The vaccine had come as far as the palace door, but it could not reach the king.

The king’s death set off another round of palace intrigue among the factions, which a few decades later led to the Kot massacre and the rise of Rana rule. After the king’s death, the three-year-old Rajendra took the throne. Lalit Tripura Sundari, who had been regent from 1806 until Girvan came of age, took up the regency again. Lalit Tripura Sundari was Bhimsen Thapa’s own niece, and under her regency Bhimsen’s grip grew even stronger. Tripura Sundari died of cholera on April 6, 1832. (Heydon, 2019)

That is when the end of Bhimsen Thapa began. After Tripura Sundari, power passed to Rajendra’s senior queen, Samrajya Laxmi, and the game of removing Bhimsen from power began. When the prince Devendra died suddenly in 1837, Bhimsen was accused of poisoning him. There has hardly been a killing as brutal as his anywhere else in Nepali history. (Baburam Acharya, Rooprekha, Ashwin 2019 BS, pp. 9–16; Stiller, 1976)

The Vaccine Arrived, but It Took 150 Years to Reach Everyone

The English hoped vaccination would spread from the Valley across the country. That did not happen. After the king’s death, vaccination began to disappear from Gardner’s letters. The campaign that began in Banepa died out there. Nor did the palace give up the old method. (Heydon, 2019)

Fourteen years after the king’s death, in 1830, there is a record of Dhiraj Vaidya being exempted from “jhara, beth and begar” (forms of compulsory labour) for variolating a prince (probably Girvan’s grandson Surendra). The palace whose door Jenner’s vaccine had reached sixteen years earlier was still relying on the old method of vaidyas and Kausas. (Regmi Research Series 7(2), 1975, p. 26; Heydon, 2019)

Jung Bahadur Rana, back from his visit to Britain in 1850, took a great interest in vaccination. Oldfield writes that Jung Bahadur had “great faith” in vaccination and had his own children and the royal children vaccinated. But he did nothing more than that. (Oldfield, 1880, vol. 1, pp. 253–54; Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020a)

The vaccine had made it inside the palace, but it never got out of the palace to the people. Until the end of the nineteenth century, the only place to be vaccinated against smallpox was the British Residency. There is a record from 1887 of people coming even from Tibet to be vaccinated at the Residency. In the early twentieth century, the British journalist Perceval Landon wrote, “Vaccination was not compulsory in Nepal, but it was free for anyone who wanted it.” (Heydon, 2019)

The vaccine had made it inside the palace, but it never got out of the palace to the people.

Most people in Nepal lived in villages, and for them simply reaching the Residency in Kathmandu was impossible. Few wanted the vaccine anyway. Some Newar and Tarai communities disliked it. At the same time, across the border in India, the British government’s vaccination campaign had gathered pace. The Rana government built no health system to take vaccination across the country.

There is only one mention of the Rana government importing vaccine: during the epidemics that followed the great earthquake of 1990 BS (1934). (Yogesh Raj and others, “Documents Related to the Early Hospitals in Nepal,” 2016) Even that was only a stopgap in a crisis. (Brahma Shumsher, Nepalko Mahabhukampa, 1992 BS; Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020c)

After 1816, until Rana rule fell, most Nepalis were no better off than before. Change began in Nepal only after Rana rule fell and democracy came in the 1950s. In the early 1960s, the Nepal government asked the World Health Organization for help controlling communicable diseases, including smallpox. In 1962, a joint smallpox control programme of the Nepal government and the WHO began in the Kathmandu Valley. (Heydon, 2019)

Faith in Shitala Maju and getting vaccinated were not opposed to each other. The WHO’s field reports show that the reasons people went unvaccinated were more practical than religious. The vaccinators arrived at exactly the times villagers were away at work in the fields or at market. People did not know they needed to be vaccinated against smallpox, and those vaccinated once did not go back for a booster. (Heydon, 2019)

Smallpox, meanwhile, was not waiting. In 1958 there was a major smallpox outbreak in Kathmandu. In 1963–64, variola major, the deadliest form of smallpox, spread from northern India and entered Nepal along the postal roads. Smallpox kept coming back every few years. A survey in 1965 found that nearly 16 percent of people in the Valley had had smallpox at some point in their lives. Those were the survivors, the ones Bachala Maju did not take. (Robertson, 2020b; Heydon, 2019)

In 1967, when Nepal joined the WHO’s smallpox eradication programme, eradication became a national effort. But even at the end of 1972, Nepal was one of only six countries in the world where smallpox remained. In 1971 the strategy changed: find it and contain it, “surveillance and containment.” (Heydon, 2019)

Health workers began going door to door looking for patients. Cash rewards were given to anyone who reported a smallpox case. Alongside this, Nepal adopted its own approach of running mass vaccination campaigns only at certain times of the year. Donald Henderson, head of the global smallpox eradication campaign, called it “new and surprisingly effective.” There is also the matter that, in the name of stopping smallpox, the government gained extraordinary powers to enter and search citizens’ homes. But it was this change of strategy after 1971 that made smallpox eradication in Nepal succeed. (Heydon, 2019; Robertson, 2020c)

Nepal’s last smallpox patient was found on April 6, 1975. After two years with no new cases anywhere, on New Year’s Day of 2034 BS (April 13, 1977), Nepal was declared free of smallpox. (Shrestha and others, The Nepal Smallpox Eradication Programme, 1977; Heydon, 2019)

The vaccine that reached the palace door in 1816 took 159 years to reach the last smallpox patient.

To protect his son from smallpox, Rana Bahadur drove sick children and their parents out of the country. Girvan, whom his father’s decree had kept from catching smallpox as a child, died of smallpox in the end. Rana Bahadur Shah had torn down Shitala Maju’s temple, and Bachala Maju took his son.

The way smallpox was finally defeated turned out to be the exact opposite: not driving the sick away but searching them out; not soldiers at every door, but health workers carrying vaccine. And the vaccine he never received drove smallpox out of Nepal a hundred and fifty-nine years after he died.

Not driving the sick away but searching them out; not soldiers at every door, but health workers carrying vaccine.

Smallpox is gone, Rana Bahadur’s palace has become a museum, the pages of the chronicles have yellowed. But the pain of the parents who, each holding half a body, gave their children to the Tamakoshi survives in no record, only in that hymn.

Every winter, when the Shitala Maju hymn is sung in the Valley, that two-hundred-year-old decree is read out once again, this time in the voice of those who were driven out.

English translation

O Shitala Maju, look at the state of your people!
Never heard of, never seen: no small child may stay in the land, so the Maharaja has decreed.
With the naykhin drum beating, with soldiers closing in, they drove the little children out.
Beaten rice tucked under the arm, children on our backs, we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
One child on the back, one on the hip, another dragged along by the hand.
Leaving Kathmandu, we spent the night in Bhaktapur and prayed to Taleju Maju.
Leaving Bhaktapur, we spent the night in Banepa and prayed to Chandeshwari.
Leaving Banepa, we spent the night in Palanchok and prayed to Bhagawati.
Leaving Palanchok, we spent the night in Dolalghat and prayed to Bhimsen.
Leaving Dolalghat, we spent the night by the Tamakoshi; we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
Leaving Dolakha, we reached the far bank of the Tamakoshi and prayed to Mahadev.
No food to eat, no clothes to wear, no shelter to call my own.
Not with whips, not with sticks, but with nettles they struck us; the soldiers closed in and drove us on.
We pray to Kachala Maju who raises the blisters, Shitala Maju who fills them with water, and Bachala Maju who takes the sick away.
If this child lives, we will set free a pair of pigeons and offer jasmine flowers of gold and silver.
In a place the sun never reaches, the child died of cold; mother and father beat their chests and wept.
The dead child may not be burned, may not be buried: look at the state of your people!
Mother holding half the body, father holding the other half, they gave the child to the Tamakoshi.
Because the Swami-king had no dharma, the children were driven out; we had to cross the Tamakoshi.
Shri Rana Bahadur, sovereign of Nepal: the people suffered greatly.
Do not, do not, Shitala Maju, we beg you a thousand times: deliver this world.

(Original text: Prem Bahadur Kansakar (ed.), Bakham Mye, 1973; Siegfried Lienhard, Songs of Nepal, 1992. The English is translated from the author’s Nepali rendering.)

Frequently asked questions

Who is Hariti or Shitala Maju?

The same goddess under different names: Hariti for Buddhists, Shitala Mata for Hindus, Ajima for Newars. In the legend, a child-eating yakshini became the protector of children after the Buddha’s lesson. Her temple stands at Swayambhu.

Why did smallpox spread in winter in Kathmandu?

The variola virus survives longer in cold, dry air. Winter was also when traders crossed the malarial Tarai from India and came down from Tibet, so movement into the Valley peaked then.

When did the smallpox vaccine reach Nepal?

In late August 1816, through the British Residency. The vaccination campaign began in Banepa on September 13 that year.

When was Nepal declared free of smallpox?

The last case was found on April 6, 1975. After two years with no new cases, Nepal was declared smallpox-free on April 13, 1977, the Nepali New Year of 2034 BS.
