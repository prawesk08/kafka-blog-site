Hadrian’s Wall and the Governors Who Wanted to Be Emperor: How Rome solved the problem of Britain- and created a far more dangerous one in doing so.

In 306 CE, a British garrison in York did something that would reshape the world: it made a man named Constantine emperor of Rome. But the story of how that happened begins not in a throne room, but in the ash layer still buried beneath London's streets. From Boudica's ruins to Hadrian's Wall to a North African governor who nearly seized the empire, Part III follows the structural trap Rome built for itself- and could never escape.

Bronze statue of Constantine the Great outside York Minster, close to where the British legions proclaimed him emperor in 306 CE.

In short

  • On 25 July 306 CE, the British legions at York proclaimed Constantine emperor; he went on to Christianize the Roman world.
  • Agricola pushed deep into Caledonia (Scotland) between 77 and 84 CE, but was recalled before he could hold it.
  • Hadrian’s Wall, begun around 122 CE, ran coast to coast. It was built to control movement more than to stop armies.
  • Holding Britain took a large army, and a large army made its governor a possible emperor: Rome’s “British Problem”.
  • In 196–197 CE, the governor Clodius Albinus stripped the Wall to fight for the throne, and lost at Lugdunum.

25 July, 306 CE, Eboracum (York)

The old emperor died at dawn. His name was Constantius, and he had ruled the western empire for a decade with weary competence. He died in a garrison city at the northern edge of the Roman world, in the cold stone fortress the Romans called Eboracum- a city on the Ouse that we now call York.

His son was at the bedside. The troops were already in the courtyard. What happened next took perhaps an hour.

The soldiers- British legions, most of them, men who had spent their careers on the Wall and in the northern forts, men who were loyal to their general and only theoretically loyal to whatever emperor sat in Rome- turned to the dead Emperor’s son, raised him on a shield, and acclaimed him Augustus- Emperor of Rome.

Colossal Portrait of Constantine I, Capitoline Museums. Fragment of a colossal marble statue of Constantine I, c.315–325 CE, Capitoline Museums, Rome.

His name was Flavius Valerius Constantinus- Constantine the Great. From that rainy courtyard in northern England, Constantine would launch a revolution. He legalized Christianity, moved the empire's capital to Constantinople, and reshaped Western civilization for the next seventeen centuries.

That world-altering history began in the mud of York. It culminated in a dangerous, insular pattern: Britain's legions had realized they had the power to make kings.

“To understand why the legions of Britain felt entitled to make an emperor without asking Rome’s permission, you have to understand what Britain had become since Boudica burned three cities to the ground - and what Hadrian’s Wall had accidentally turned it into.”

I. The Roman Britain Rebuilt- Britain After the Ash

Fishbourne Roman Palace mosaic, West Sussex. The dolphin and cupid mosaic at Fishbourne Roman Palace, West Sussex, c.75 CE.

The fire layer is still there. Archaeologists call it the Boudican destruction horizon. Half a meter beneath the modern streets of London lies a permanent band of black, charred earth- the physical residue of the year 61 CE, when Queen Boudica burned Rome’s initial colony to the ground.

It is not an abstraction. It is the physical residue of how close this province came to being lost entirely. But Rome did not lose it.

The governor Suetonius Paulinus crushed Boudica’s force in a single set-piece battle. Then he went on punishing the rebel tribes so thoroughly that his own superiors in Rome grew alarmed. When the new procurator wrote to the emperor complaining that the governor’s scorched-earth policy was destroying the tax base the province existed to provide, Paulinus was recalled.

It was a characteristically Roman response: military victory followed by administrative adjustment. The governor who replaced Paulinus was more conciliatory, and he initiated a more restrained policy. The tribes that had not rebelled were rewarded. The tribes that had rebelled were carefully, deliberately brought back into the provincial economy.

Over the next few decades, southern Britain underwent a dramatic transformation. Londinium was rebuilt grander than before, with a new forum larger than any other north of the Alps, a governor’s palace on the Thames waterfront, and public baths. Camulodunum rose with a new defensive wall- the oldest surviving Roman town wall in Britain, still standing in Colchester today.

In the south, the villas multiplied: mosaic floors, hypocaust heating, olive oil from Spain, wine from Gaul, glassware from Syria. A Briton born in the decade of Boudica’s rebellion could, by old age, look out from a colonnaded townhouse at a landscape so Roman that the torched roundhouses of their childhood might have belonged to a different world entirely.

But that comfortable, Romanized south rested on a foundation that was never fully stable. Because north and west of the new towns and villas, the province had a problem it could not solve by building better roads.

II. The Edge That Would Not Yield - Agricola’s Push North

Gnaeus Julius Agricola arrived as governor of Britain in 77 CE and spent the next seven years doing something no Roman governor had quite managed before: he was patient. He did not simply march north and force a battle. He built roads as he went. He established supply lines. He fortified as he advanced. By the standards of Roman frontier commanders, he was methodical to the point of being unusual.

Agricola’s campaigns in Britain, 78–84 CE. Map of Governor Gnaeus Julius Agricola’s northward advance, 77–84 CE. He reached further into Caledonia than any Roman general before or after him, and was recalled before he could consolidate it. The territory he abandoned would define Rome’s limits in Britain for the next three centuries.

He pacified the remaining tribes of Wales. By 83 CE, he had pushed the legions deep into Scotland's rugged highlands, smashing the Caledonian tribes at the Battle of Mons Graupius.

But a suspicious emperor abruptly recalled Agricola to Rome; the advance stalled, and the far northern frontier devolved into a violent, unpredictable border zone. The northern forts were thinned. The advance stalled. The province contracted. During this volatile period, around 120 CE, the battle-hardened Ninth Legion mysteriously vanished from the historical record.

Hadrian became emperor in 117 CE and arrived in Britain in 122 CE to assess the problem himself. What he found convinced him that the far north could not be held. He decided that the wild Scottish Highlands were not worth the endless cost in Roman blood.

He chose to draw a permanent line in stone. He built a wall that still stands today.

III. Hadrian’s Wall- The Empire Draws Its Line

Hadrian’s Wall, Northumberland. Hadrian’s Wall running west across the Northumberland landscape- 73 Roman miles of stone, begun in 122 CE and taking eight years to complete.

Hadrian’s Wall was begun around 122 CE and took roughly eight years to complete. It ran 73 Roman miles- about 80 modern miles- coast to coast across the narrowest neck of the island.

At its core, it was a wall: up to ten feet wide, originally perhaps fifteen feet high, built from dressed stone quarried from the surrounding landscape.

But the Wall itself was only one layer of a system. In front of it, a wide ditch. Behind it, a military road giving rapid north-south movement. Further south still, a second earthwork barrier called the Vallum marked the limit of the military zone. Every Roman mile, a fortified gateway. Every five to eight miles, a full fort could hold a cohort of five hundred or more soldiers. Observation towers were placed between every milecastle. Signal systems to pass messages coast to coast in minutes.

The Wall was not primarily designed to stop an army. The Wall was designed to control. It controlled who moved, what they carried, where they went, and what they paid in tolls and taxes. Every gate was a checkpoint. The whole system was customs and intelligence infrastructure as much as military fortification- Rome’s administrative logic carved into the northern landscape.

The Wall worked, in the narrow sense: the province south of it was never again overrun, as Boudica had nearly done.

Vindolanda writing tablets, c.85–130 CE. The Vindolanda tablets, British Museum- the oldest surviving handwritten documents in Britain. Duty rosters, grain inventories, a birthday invitation, a soldier’s request for more socks against the northern cold. The Wall in the soldiers’ own words.

But maintaining it required a massive standing army at the edge of the earth, creating a far more dangerous problem than the architects of Roman Britannia had planned for.

IV. The British Paradox - Why Britain’s Strength Was Its Downfall

Here is the problem at the heart of Roman Britain, the problem that no emperor or governor ever fully solved, the problem that runs from Boudica’s ash to the moment the troops in that York courtyard raised Constantine on a shield.

Britain required a large army to govern. The island was geographically isolated- cut off from the rest of the empire by the Channel, with a permanently restive north that needed constant garrisoning.

A weak military presence meant what it had meant under Boudica: a province that could be overrun from within. Rome needed soldiers here. Lots of them.

But a large army meant a powerful commander. Operating at the end of the longest supply line in the Western world, the Governor of Britain functioned less like a politician and more like an autonomous monarch.

“In normal times, when Rome was stable, and the emperor was strong, and there was no civil war to tempt a general’s ambition, this arrangement functioned. The governor governed. The army garrisoned. The taxes were collected. The Wall was maintained. The times were not always normal.”

V. Albinus- The First March

Marble bust, Severan period, c.193–211 CE. Marble portrait bust from the Severan period, late 2nd to early 3rd century CE.

Roman historians refer to 193 CE as the Year of the Five Emperors.

The emperor Commodus had been strangled on New Year’s Eve of 192 CE. His replacement, Pertinax, lasted eighty-seven days before being murdered by the Praetorian Guard. The Guard then auctioned the office of emperor to the highest bidder. A wealthy senator named Didius Julianus won. He lasted sixty-six days.

Into this chaos stepped three provincial governors, each with a large army and a legitimate claim to have had enough. One was Pescennius Niger in the east. One was Septimius Severus, commander of the Danubian legions. And the other was Decimus Clodius Albinus, governor of Britain.

Albinus was himself a product of Rome's remarkable geographic reach. He was born in Hadrumetum, a prosperous port city on the North African coast in what is now Tunisia, to a Roman family long settled in the province of Africa Proconsularis. He had grown up under the wide skies and warm sun of the Mediterranean world, far removed from the grey northern frontier he would eventually be sent to govern.

He had served in military posts across the empire before receiving Britain, and he was known, unusually for a frontier commander, as a man of literary cultivation. The Historia Augusta credits him with writing pastoral fables in his spare time. Whether the cold rain of Britannia suited a man from the warmth of North Africa is not recorded. What is clear is that he understood the province he inherited, and he governed it, for the first years, with efficiency and genuine care.

Civil war erupted in Rome. Albinus was not a fool. He recognized that he could not fight both Niger and Severus simultaneously. When Severus offered him the title of Caesar, junior emperor, Albinus accepted.

Coin of Clodius Albinus, c.193–197 CE. Aureus of Clodius Albinus, governor of Britain, struck c.193–197 CE. Born in Hadrumetum, North Africa, he governed Britain with competence and loyalty- until the moment he had to choose between obedience and the throne.

It was a trap. By 196 CE, Severus had eliminated his eastern rivals and turned his sights on Britain. Knowing his life was forfeit, Albinus made his move. He crossed the Channel to claim the supreme title of Augustus.

This bought Albinus time; he stabilized Britain and kept his options open while Severus disposed of Niger in the east. By 196 CE, with Niger defeated, it was clear that Severus had no intention of honoring the arrangement.

Albinus made his move. To win a continental war, Albinus made the catastrophic decision to strip the island bare. Forts along Hadrian’s Wall were abandoned, and the entire permanent garrison of Britain was ferried to Gaul.

By early 197 CE, Albinus controlled Gaul, Spain, and Britain. Severus marched west to meet him. The armies of Albinus and Severus collided in one of the bloodiest conflicts in Roman history at Lugdunum, modern Lyon, on 19 February 197 CE. It was one of the largest battles fought on Western European soil since the Republic's fall. Over 150,000 Roman citizens slaughtered each other.

Albinus’s British legions fought with ferocious bravery, nearly breaking Severus’s lines, but a decisive cavalry charge broke the British flank. Albinus’s army was systematically butchered. Defeated, the British governor fled to a nearby house and fell on his sword.

The British army that had followed him was decimated. The province that had produced him was stripped of soldiers, left exposed on its northern frontier, and governed for the next generation by men chosen for their loyalty rather than their competence.

VI. The Legacy of the Flame

Victorian commemorative mural, Governors of Roman Britain. A Victorian-era mural depicting the great governors and figures of Roman Britain: Septimius Severus, Antoninus Pius, Hadrian, Agricola, and Tacitus.

Back on the lonely northern frontier of Britannia, Albinus’s ambition was paid for in ash. The native tribes realized the Wall was empty. They poured south across the undefended border, leaving a stark archaeological layer of burning, looting, and destruction across the northern forts. Rome took years to rebuild the line.

In 208 CE, the aging, gout-ridden Emperor Septimius Severus personally traveled to Britain to crush the northern tribes. He launched brutal, exhausting scorched-earth campaigns into Scotland, but the relentless guerrilla warfare broke his remaining health.

He withdrew south to the military headquarters at Eboracum and died within the stone walls of York in 211 CE.

The template of Roman Britannia was set. The island was no longer just a difficult frontier. Britain was not just a difficult province to govern. It was a province that, in moments of Roman weakness, would act. Albinus had proven that the governor of Britain- with his wall, his isolated legions, and his island kingdom- could look at a fractured empire and make a credible play for the throne of the world.

This was what Roman called the British Problem. The governors were warlords of an island kingdom. And warlords, when the throne of the world becomes available, have a habit of reaching for it.

Albinus was the first. He would not be the last.

Others would notice. A century later, in that very same city of York, Constantine would succeed.
