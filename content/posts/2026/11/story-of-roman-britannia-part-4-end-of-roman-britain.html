How Rome left Britain, and what it left behind- The End of Roman Britannia and the work of the giants.

In 410 CE, an emperor hiding in Ravenna sent a letter to Britain's cities with a simple message: look to your own defense. But Rome's exit isn't a sudden collapse; it is a tale of ambitious usurpers slowly bleeding the island of its legions to fight for the throne.

From the elevation of an ordinary soldier to the arrival of Anglo-Saxons who wandered through the ruins, Part IV explores the melancholic end of an empire and the "work of giants" it left behind.

Coin of Constantine III, 407–411 CE. An ordinary soldier elevated by the British legions because of his name.

In short

  • From Carausius in 287 CE to Magnus Maximus in 383 CE, Britain’s armies kept crossing the Channel to make emperors.
  • After 306 CE, Constantine the Great took British legions east. They were never fully replaced, but Christianity stayed.
  • In 407 CE, the legions chose a soldier named Constantine. Constantine III took the last garrison to Gaul and was executed in 411.
  • Around 410 CE, Emperor Honorius told Britain’s cities to defend themselves. Roman Britain ended with a letter, not a battle.
  • Anglo-Saxon settlers found the ruins and, in the Old English poem The Ruin, called them enta geweorc, “the work of giants”.

407 CE: A Military Camp in Northern Britannia

It had happened before. That was the point.

The legions of Britain had done this twice already in the past year alone. They had raised a soldier named Marcus on a shield and called him emperor. When Marcus disappointed them, they killed him and raised a man named Gratian.

When Gratian disappointed them within four months, they killed him too. Now they needed a third candidate.

The Western Empire was collapsing around them. Barbarians had crossed the frozen Rhine on the last day of 406 CE in the largest single migration the empire had ever seen. Gaul was burning. The legitimate emperor, Honorius, had retreated behind the impregnable marshes of Ravenna and showed no sign of coming out.

The British legions looked at the chaos across the Channel and made a decision. They wanted a name. Not a strategy. Not a general of proven brilliance. A name.

A century earlier, in this very province, a man named Constantine had been raised on a shield by British legions, and that Constantine had gone on to conquer the entire Roman world. The soldiers of Britain had developed something close to a superstition about it.

The name felt like a formula. Raise a Constantine, win the world.

They looked around the camp. They found a soldier. An ordinary soldier, nobody famous, nobody distinguished, no military record that survives in any source. But his name was Constantine.

They raised him on a shield.

He immediately stripped the last garrison from the island, crossed the Channel, and was dead within four years.

Britain never got its soldiers back, and Roman Britannia died with Constantine the Third.

The formula had worked once. That was the problem. Once is enough to make a tradition, and traditions in the Roman army had a way of outlasting their reasons.

I. The Pattern: A Century of Warlords

Coin of Carausius, self-proclaimed Emperor of the Britannic Empire, 287–293 CE. A naval commander who held Britain and northern Gaul against Rome for nearly a decade.

The century between Albinus and Constantine III had confirmed, again and again, that Britain was less a province than a launching pad.

After the catastrophe of 197 CE, after Albinus’s army had been butchered at Lugdunum and the northern Wall stripped bare, it took the personal intervention of the emperor Septimius Severus to stabilize the province. He arrived in 208 CE, old and gouty, and launched brutal punitive campaigns deep into Scotland.

Septimius died in York in 211 CE, the same city where Constantine would later be proclaimed. His sons Caracalla and Geta promptly abandoned their father’s Scottish ambitions and made peace with the northern tribes.

However, the damage had been done. The northern tribes had seen the Wall empty. They burned the forts and proved the frontier was permeable. Rome had lost the psychological advantage it had spent decades building.

In 260 CE, a breakaway Gallic Empire briefly incorporated Britain into its territories, three separate emperors governing a rump western empire for fifteen years, while the legitimate court struggled in the east.

In 287 CE came the most audacious episode of all: a naval commander named Carausius, facing prosecution for corruption, declared himself emperor of Britain and northern Gaul. He issued his own coins. He maintained his own court. He controlled the Channel fleet and defied Rome for seven years until his own minister had him murdered.

His successor lasted another three years before the emperor Constantius, father of Constantine the Great, mounted a military expedition and brought Britain back under central control.

Each episode followed the same grammar. A strong commander. A large army. A fractured empire. A crossing of the Channel. And Britain, once again, was left to manage itself in the interim, stripped of soldiers, exposed to the north, working out its own arrangements.

The island was becoming adept at self-sufficiency. That would matter in the end.

II. The Emperor from the Island: Constantine and the Christian World

Chi-Rho mosaic, Hinton St Mary, Dorset, c.350 CE, British Museum. The earliest known image of Christ from Roman Britain.

Constantine the Great, proclaimed in York in 306 CE, changed the world. But what did he do for Britain?

The British Problem demanded it. Constantine could not stay in Britain and be content. The very pattern that had made him emperor made staying impossible. The Tetrarchy, the system of four co-emperors Diocletian designed to prevent exactly this kind of provincial coup, had given legitimate succession to Galerius, the senior Augustus in the east.

Statue of Constantine: a bronze statue of Constantine the Great in York, the very city where he was proclaimed emperor in 306 CE before marching south to claim the Roman world.

Galerius had not appointed Constantine. The British legions had. That made Constantine a usurper under Roman law, regardless of his father’s bloodline. His only path to legitimacy was conquest.

He had to take the whole empire or lose his head. The same isolation that made Britain a breeding ground for emperor-makers also made it a trap: a man proclaimed in York could be dismissed as a provincial warlord unless he moved fast, moved south, and proved himself in the arena that actually counted.

The British Problem, in other words, did not just create Constantine. It chased him out of Britain. The province that handed him his purple had to strip its legions to fund the campaign that would make him real. Britain lost its emperor the same way it always did, by having him cross the Channel and never come back.

In the short term, he left. Britain was the platform, not the destination. He took his British legions east, fought his way across Gaul and Italy, and in 312 CE at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome, defeated his last serious rival under the sign of the Chi-Rho, the Christian symbol he claimed had appeared to him in a vision the night before battle. He legalized Christianity, convened the Council of Nicaea, founded Constantinople, and became the most consequential emperor since Augustus.

Britain received Christianity in return. The faith had been spreading quietly through the province for decades. Roman Britain shifted from a province with a patchwork of mystery cults, local gods, and a growing Christian minority to a province with an established church.

Christianity would prove to be the most durable thing Rome left in Britain. Constantine’s conversion made it official.

Christian communities existed in the larger towns, and British bishops attended the Council of Arles in 314 CE. Long after the administrators left, the coins stopped coming, and the villas were stripped for building stone, the church remained.

But the legions Constantine took east in 306 CE were never fully replaced. The garrison thinned. The Wall was still manned, the towns still functioning, the villas still occupied, but the depth of military resources that made Britain feel like a functioning part of the empire was quietly, steadily ebbing.

III. Magnus Maximus: The Second March

Coin portrait of Magnus Maximus, proclaimed emperor by the British legions in 383 CE. Remembered in Wales as Macsen Wledig, the last Roman who felt like theirs.

The clearest preview of the end came in 383 CE.

Gratian was the son of Valentinian I, a competent soldier-emperor who had kept the western frontiers intact through sheer professional force of will. When Valentinian died in 375 CE, power in the west passed to Gratian, who was sixteen. He grew into a ruler not without ability, but he inherited an empire in crisis and possessed none of his father’s iron authority.

The catastrophe arrived in 378 CE. The Goths, granted refuge inside the Danube frontier by a corrupt Roman administration, rose in revolt. At the Battle of Adrianople, the eastern emperor Valens, Gratian’s uncle, rode into a Gothic ambush and was killed. The Roman army of the east was effectively destroyed. It was the Roman Empire's worst military defeat at the time.

What followed hardened the western legions' contempt for Gratian. He responded to the Gothic crisis not by crushing the Goths but by negotiating with them, granting them unprecedented terms in 382 CE that allowed them to settle as a semi-autonomous people and owe Rome only loose military service.

To soldiers who had spent their careers dying to hold the line, this looked like surrender. Worse still, in the eyes of the traditional army, was what Gratian surrounded himself with. He showed marked personal favor to a unit of Alanic cavalry, steppe nomads; dressing in their style, hunting with them, lavishing them with gifts. To the Roman soldiery, this was not merely eccentric. It was a humiliation. Their emperor preferred barbarians to them.

In Britain, Magnus Maximus watched all of this from the far end of the empire. He was a capable, experienced Spaniard, deeply popular with the troops who had served under him for years along the Wall. When the chance came, his soldiers made the familiar calculation and proclaimed him Augustus.

End of Roman Britannia, 383–410 CE.

Maximus crossed to Gaul, defeated and killed Gratian, and established himself as western emperor. He ruled competently for five years, governing from Trier, negotiating with the Franks and Visigoths, and holding the Rhine frontier against relentless pressure.

By most measures, Maximus was a better emperor than the man he replaced. But Maximus stripped Britain’s garrison to fight his continental war, and that came with a heavy price.

And in 388 CE, the eastern emperor Theodosius marched west, defeated him in battle, and had him executed. The troops Maximus had taken from Britain never returned.

The Western Empire he left behind was unstable and diminished. Gratian’s younger brother Valentinian II was restored as western emperor in name, but Theodosius held the real power. When Valentinian II was found dead in 392 CE, almost certainly murdered by his powerful Frankish general Arbogast, the general installed a pagan professor named Eugenius as a puppet emperor and governed through him.

Theodosius marched west again. At the Battle of the Frigidus in 394 CE, he defeated Arbogast and Eugenius in one of the century's most bitterly fought engagements. Eugenius was executed. Arbogast fell on his sword. Theodosius became, briefly, the last man to rule the entire Roman Empire alone.

He died in 395 CE, leaving the empire to his two young sons. Arcadius, seventeen, received the east. Honorius, ten years old, received the west. The division was supposed to be administrative, not permanent. It proved to be both. The empire was never reunified.

And the soldiers that Maximus had taken from Britain were gone, absorbed into continental wars, deployed along the Rhine and the Danube, buried on Italian hillsides. Britain would make do with what was left.

There was always somewhere the soldiers were needed more. The eastern frontier. The Danube. The Rhine. Gaul. Spain. Italy. Britain waited at the end of the list, and the list never got shorter.

IV. The Last Constantine: 407 CE

The Western Roman Empire, c.410 CE. Britain sits at the fraying edge of a world dissolving from within. Honorius told its cities to defend themselves. No reinforcements were promised. No governor was named. That was the last official communication.

Which brings us back to the ordinary soldier with the fortunate name.

By 407 CE, the Britain that remained was diminished. The great villa estates of the south were fewer and quieter than they had been a century before. Coin circulation had been steadily declining since the 360s.

The towns still stood. The Wall was still, nominally, garrisoned. The administrative structures of the Diocese of Britannia still functioned. But the connection to Rome was fraying at every edge, and everyone in the province who paid attention knew it.

Constantine III crossed to Gaul in 407 CE with what remained of Britain’s garrison. He had early success; he stabilized the Rhine frontier, smashed the previous winter, gathered support in Gaul and Spain, and for a moment looked like he might be the real thing. He controlled more territory than most of his predecessors.

Coins of Constantine III.

But 407 CE was also the year the Visigoths under Alaric were circling Italy. And in 410 CE came the event that stunned the Roman world: Alaric sacked Rome itself. The eternal city, symbol of everything the empire meant, fell to a barbarian army for the first time in eight hundred years. The psychological shock was total.

Jerome wrote from Bethlehem that he could not speak for tears. Augustine began writing The City of God, an entire theology designed to explain why Rome's fall did not mean the fall of meaning.

By 411 CE, Honorius's forces had captured and executed Constantine III. The soldiers he had taken from Britain were dead, dispersed, or absorbed into other units.

The Diocese of Britannia, four provinces, administered from London, garrisoned along Hadrian’s Wall, integrated into the tax system and economy of the western empire for nearly four centuries, quietly ceased to function as a Roman entity.

In that same catastrophic year of 410 CE, the emperor Honorius sent a letter to the cities of Britain. The letter told the British cities to defend themselves.

No reinforcements were promised. No new governor was appointed. No timeline was given for restoring Roman authority. In the precise, bureaucratic language of imperial administration, the letter told the cities of Britain they were on their own.

That was all. This is how Roman Britannia ended. Not with a battle. Not with an invasion.

With a letter. With silence. With the slow absence of the coins that no longer arrived on the supply ships that no longer came.

V. The Silence and What Followed

Portchester Castle, Hampshire, a Saxon Shore fort built by the Romans c.285 CE to defend against Germanic raiders.

The fall of Roman Britain was not an event. It was a slow, uneven process, stretching across generations and visible only in retrospect.

The towns did not immediately empty. The villas did not immediately collapse. The roads kept working because roads keep working; they require no maintenance to remain usable for decades.

The Christian church survived better than the imperial administration that had legitimized it. The language shifted gradually, British Latin giving way to the older Celtic tongues, then, over the following centuries, to the Germanic dialects of newcomers from across the North Sea.

The newcomers had been coming for years. Germanic warriors- Angles, Saxons, Jutes, Frisians- had been raiding the eastern coast of Britain for generations.

The Romans had called this coastline the Saxon Shore and built a chain of massive forts specifically to defend against them. Now no one manned the forts. The walls still stood. The gates still swung. But the garrison was gone, and the coast was open.

Some came as raiders. Some came as settlers, invited by sub-Roman British rulers who needed warriors and had land to offer. Some came simply because the land was available and no organized military force was defending it. The process took generations, not years. In the west, Wales, Cornwall, Cumbria, parts of the north, Romano-British culture persisted for a century or more after the administrative end of Roman rule.

But across the lowland east, the heartland of Roman Britain, the land of villas and forums and heated floors and colonnaded basilicas, the transformation was eventually total.

The towns were gradually abandoned or occupied in ways that left no trace in the Roman building stock. The villas fell into disrepair, their roofs collapsing inward, their mosaic floors buried under the slow accumulation of rubble, soil, and time. The forum of Londinium, once the largest north of the Alps, became a field.

And then the Anglo-Saxons walked among the ruins.

VI. The Work of Giants

The Great Bath, Aquae Sulis (Bath), Somerset. The sacred hot springs channeled, roofed, and monumentalized by Rome. The steam never stopped rising. The people who built the roof were gone before anyone thought to write their names down.

They didn't know who built them.

The Anglo-Saxon settlers who moved through the abandoned landscapes of what had been Roman Britain looked at the stone walls, the crumbling basilicas, the bathhouses with their mysterious underfloor cavities, and the great roads running straight and true to the horizon, with no framework for understanding any of it.

Nothing in their world was built from dressed stone. Nothing in their experience suggested that human hands could have raised structures on this scale.

They called them enta geweorc. The work of giants.

An anonymous Anglo-Saxon poet, writing sometime in the 8th or 9th century, three hundred years after the last Roman administrator had left, stood before the ruins of Roman Bath, Aquae Sulis, and tried to put into words what they saw.

The poem has come down to us damaged and incomplete, half-eaten by time, missing whole passages. We call it The Ruin. What survives is one of the most quietly devastating things ever written in English.

Wrætlic is þes wealstan, wyrde gebræcon,
burgstede burston, brosnað enta geweorc.
Wondrous is this masonry, broken by fate,
the city-places have crumbled, the work of giants decays.

The poet described roofless halls and fallen towers. The frost on the stones. The place where men had once laughed and drunk and glittered with gold. They described the hot springs still steaming, still running, still warm, three centuries after the last Roman had used them, as though the earth itself remembered what had been built above it and refused to forget.

The poet had no word for Roman. No concept of the empire that had built this, governed this, garrisoned this, taxed this, Christianized this, and then, exhaustedly, bureaucratically, with a letter nobody quite remembers sending, let go of this.

They saw only the evidence of a greatness they could not name, in a language that carried no memory of what had made it. And they wrote it down.

That act, the Anglo-Saxon poet standing in the ruins and recording their wonder at the work of giants, is the strange, melancholy full stop at the end of Roman Britain. Four centuries of roads and walls and law and language and religion, of olive oil from Spain and wine from Gaul and letters home complaining about the cold, reduced in human memory to the evidence of a vanished race of builders.

Rome came to Britain not to liberate it, not to love it, not to stay. It came to extract, to garrison, to tax, and in the end, to leave. What it built in the leaving was something it never intended: a people practiced in surviving without it.

What remained, the roads still running, the walls still standing, the hot springs still steaming, the church still singing in Latin to congregations who no longer spoke it, the genes and place-names and legal habits and crop rotations quietly absorbed into the fabric of an island, was the ghost of an occupation that had lasted longer than the United States has yet existed.

The Anglo-Saxon poet stood in the ruins and marveled at the work of giants.

We are still, in some sense, standing in it.
