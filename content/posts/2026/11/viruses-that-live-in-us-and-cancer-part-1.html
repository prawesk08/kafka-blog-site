Many of us think of cancer as a disease caused by cigarettes, alcohol, pesticides in our food, or errors in DNA passed down through families. All of these are causes of cancer. But they are not the only ones.

There is another cause, one many of us may not know about: viral infection. It may sound surprising, but roughly one in every eight new cancers in the world is caused by some kind of infection.

Most viruses that cause colds and coughs do not stay in our bodies for good. The infection happens, the virus multiplies, our immune system brings it under control, and after a while we recover. But not every virus has that story. Some viruses, once they infect us, can hide in our bodies for years, and in some cases settle in for life.

In short

  • Roughly one in eight new cancers in the world is caused by an infection.
  • Cancer is cells dividing at will, after errors pile up in their DNA.
  • In 1909–11, Peyton Rous found a cancer-causing virus in a chicken’s tumour.
  • In 1964, Epstein, Achong and Barr found EBV, the first virus linked to cancer in humans.
  • Carrying a virus is not the same as having cancer: T cells keep most of us safe.

Cervical cancer, liver cancer, stomach cancer and a few others are examples. Some cancers are caused by viruses and some by bacteria. But having a virus in the body and having cancer are not the same thing.

So how does an ordinary virus hiding in the body bring on a disease as serious as cancer? Before we can answer that, we need to understand how cancer arises inside our bodies.

What Is Cancer?

Our bodies are made of tens of trillions of cells. Inside every cell is an instruction manual called DNA. When a cell grows, when it divides, when it does its job and when it dies: all of this is regulated by the genes in the DNA.

For example, if you cut your hand, the cells around the wound divide. New cells form around the wound and fill it in. Once the wound has closed, cell division stops. In this way the body keeps a balance between making cells and removing them. Cells divide when needed and stop once the work is done. The instructions for what to do and when come from the DNA inside the cell itself.

If the DNA changes for some reason, the system that drives cell division and the system that controls it can go wrong. The signal that should normally stop cell division may stop working, or signals urging the cell to keep dividing may switch on. If the system that repairs DNA damage also breaks down, such changes keep piling up.

As errors in the DNA accumulate this way, cells go rogue. They keep dividing, form a tumour (a lump), and begin spreading to other parts of the body. That is what cancer is: cells dividing at will.

Cigarette smoke, the sun’s ultraviolet rays and some chemicals can damage DNA in this way. These outside agents that raise the risk of cancer are called carcinogens. But not every cause of change in DNA has to come from outside.

Errors can also happen while DNA is being copied as a cell divides. Our bodies make billions of new cells every day, and each time the entire DNA has to be copied anew. Our cells are very skilled at this, but copy a book of thousands of pages countless times and a letter is bound to slip somewhere.

In the same way, some viruses can hijack the very system that makes a cell divide: some change the activity of particular genes, some insert their own DNA into ours. And some viruses, even if they cannot get into our DNA, can hide for life in various cells of the body. Some cause constant inflammation in the cells where they hide, creating conditions that favour cancer. Some lie dormant for a lifetime, waking only if the body’s immune system falters, and then they set the cell they were hiding in dividing out of control.

Viruses like these, with the capacity to cause cancer in our bodies, are called oncoviruses. They do not all work the same way, but when they become active, the result is that the cell’s regulation breaks down and cancer can form.

The discovery that viruses cause cancer did not begin with a human being. The search for cancer-causing viruses began with a chicken.

A Cancer-Causing Virus Found in a Chicken’s Tumour

In 1909, an American farmer brought a chicken with a tumour growing on its breast to Peyton Rous, a doctor in New York. Rous removed the tumour and ground it up. He passed it through a filter so fine that not even bacteria could get through, and injected the clear filtered liquid into another, healthy chicken.

A few weeks later, the healthy chicken grew the same kind of tumour.

This was no ordinary experiment. The liquid made from the ground-up tumour contained no tumour cells. The bacteria had been filtered out too. And yet the disease had passed on. So Rous concluded that the agent causing the tumour might be an extremely small particle he could not see, small enough to pass through the filter.

That agent was later confirmed to be a virus. It was named Rous sarcoma virus. But the scientists of the day were not ready to connect this chicken experiment with human cancer. It took a long time for the idea that viruses could cause cancer to become established. Rous, who showed back in 1911 that a virus causes cancer, had to wait another 55 years for his Nobel Prize.

The story of how viruses themselves were discovered is similar. Towards the end of the nineteenth century, tobacco farmers in Europe were plagued by tobacco mosaic disease, which left mottled spots on the leaves. Grinding up a diseased leaf and applying it to a healthy plant passed the disease on. But under the microscope, no bacterium that could be the cause was ever found.

In 1892, the Russian scientist Dmitri Ivanovsky passed the sap of a diseased leaf through a filter that bacteria could not get through. When the filtered sap still spread the disease, he raised the possibility that the cause might be smaller than an ordinary bacterium. In 1898, the Dutch scientist Martinus Beijerinck repeated the same experiment and concluded that this was something different from bacteria, a new kind of disease-causing agent, and he called it a “virus.”

Virus is a Latin word meaning poison. Rous later used the same method on the chicken tumour. But people had to wait until 1939, when an electron microscope photographed tobacco mosaic virus, to see a virus with their own eyes for the first time.

The First Cancer Virus Found in Humans

In the 1950s, Denis Burkitt, a doctor working in Uganda, noticed an aggressive cancer that made children’s jaws swell abnormally. When he mapped where in Africa the disease was most common, he found a geographic pattern. The cancer was more common in hot, rainy areas, with lots of mosquitoes and widespread malaria.

Burkitt guessed that some infection carried by mosquitoes might be linked to the disease. The jaw cancer he saw is now called Burkitt lymphoma. It was only a few years later that people learned the cause was not that mosquitoes directly carry a cancer-causing virus, but a complex relationship between malaria and another virus.

When Burkitt presented his findings in London in 1961, Anthony Epstein, who was searching for the cause of the disease, was in the audience. He asked Burkitt for cancer samples and began his research.

In 1964, looking through an electron microscope, he and his colleagues Bert Achong and Yvonne Barr found a kind of virus inside the cancer cells. Today we call this first virus linked to cancer in humans the Epstein–Barr virus, or EBV for short.

Today a great deal is understood about the role EBV plays in the human body. But before its story, one important point is worth considering: a virus infecting the body, a virus settling in our cells, and cancer arising from it are three different events.

How Are Oncoviruses Different from Other Viruses?

Viruses like EBV that can cause cancer are called oncoviruses. The Greek word “onkos” means a tumour or lump: a virus that can make tumours. So far, seven viruses have been firmly linked to cancer in humans. But before we can understand them, we need to understand what other viruses are like.

A virus by itself is a lifeless particle; it cannot live or multiply on its own. For that it needs a living cell. Only by entering a living cell and using that cell’s machinery can it make copies of itself.

The viruses of colds, influenza or COVID are short-stay guests. They enter a cell, make many copies, burst the cell and kill it, and leave at once in search of the next person. Then the body’s immune system brings them under control and drives them out within a few days. The effects of the infection, and the immune system’s memory of it, do stay for a long time.

But some viruses, like EBV of the herpes family, can keep their genetic material (DNA) in the body’s cells in an inactive state after infection. They do not kill the cell right away; instead they settle inside it and stay for years. This state is called latent infection. Such a virus can reactivate from time to time, although it does not always cause symptoms.

For viruses like these, more cells means more homes. That is why they push cells to divide, like loosening a car’s brakes and pressing the accelerator. Some, like the hepatitis viruses, do not do this; instead they keep up years of injury and inflammation in the liver and other organs, smoothing the road to cancer. Not all cancer-causing viruses work the same way.

But a virus settling in the body is not enough on its own to cause cancer. Our immune system has cells called T cells that act as the body’s guards. They are always on the lookout for cells behaving abnormally, and kill them as soon as they find them.

That is why, even though many of us carry oncoviruses, most of us never get cancer. Cancer happens only when more errors pile up in a cell the virus is egging on, and the immune system fails to catch the damaged cells. That is why virus-caused cancers usually appear decades after infection, mostly in old age, when the body’s guards have grown tired.

Interestingly, cancer does the virus no good. Cancer itself is not a way for the virus to spread; cancer does not carry the virus to another person. For the virus, cancer is just an accident.

Most people in the world carry EBV, but only a few get cancer. Besides EBV, six other viruses and one bacterium that we know of can cause cancer. In the next part I will write about these cancer-causing viruses that live in almost all of us, the cancers they cause, and how to protect ourselves.

Frequently asked questions

Can viruses cause cancer?

Some can. They are called oncoviruses. Roughly one in eight new cancers worldwide is linked to an infection.

What was the first human cancer virus discovered?

The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), found in 1964 by Anthony Epstein, Bert Achong and Yvonne Barr in samples of Burkitt lymphoma.

How many oncoviruses are known?

Seven have been firmly linked to cancer in humans: EBV, KSHV, HPV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HTLV-1 and Merkel cell polyomavirus.

Does carrying a cancer virus mean you will get cancer?

No. In most people, T cells keep infected cells in check. Cancer develops only when more errors build up and the immune system fails to catch the damaged cells.
