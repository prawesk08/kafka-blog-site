About 50 years after we learned that a virus causes cancer in chickens, a virus was shown for the first time to cause cancer in humans too. In the 1950s, a doctor working in Uganda noticed a strange cancer that made the jaw swell. At first he suspected malaria might be behind it.

But a few years later, when another team of scientists examined samples of the same cancer, they found the cause was a virus. The virus was named the Epstein–Barr virus, after two of the scientists on the team that discovered it.

Known as EBV for short, it was the first cancer-causing virus found in humans. The interesting thing is that EBV has settled in the bodies of most of us, for life.

In short

  • More than 90% of adults carry EBV, but T cells keep it in check for life.
  • Immune-suppressing drugs after an organ transplant can let EBV wake up and cause a lymphoma called PTLD.
  • With age, DNA errors pile up and fewer new guards arrive, which is why most cancers appear after 50 or 60.
  • HPV causes cervical cancer; Nepal began vaccinating girls against it in 2025.
  • The hepatitis B vaccine was the world’s first cancer-preventing vaccine, and Nepal gives it routinely to infants.

EBV: In Almost Everyone, Cancer in Only a Few

After EBV was found, scientists tested the blood of people all over the world and learned something surprising: almost everyone carries this virus. More than 90 percent of adults in the world have EBV in their bodies.

EBV spreads mainly through saliva. In our society, where children eat from the same plate and drink from the same glass, most people catch the virus young without ever knowing. When the first infection comes in the teenage or adult years, some people get fever, a sore throat, swollen lymph nodes and long-lasting fatigue. Because it spreads through contact with saliva, it is also called the “kissing disease.”

Once it has infected us, EBV never leaves our bodies. It hides for life inside a kind of white blood cell, the B cell. In Africa it causes cancer in children’s jaws, and in southern China and Southeast Asia, cancer at the back of the nose. EBV is also linked to some stomach cancers, skin cancers and cancers of the lymph nodes (lymphoma).

In most people, though, it stays asleep for life. The body’s immune system keeps the hidden virus and its effects under control for a very long time. As soon as a cell carrying the virus tries to divide abnormally, T cells recognise it and kill it. This balance between the virus and the guards goes on for decades, without our ever knowing.

In Burkitt’s children, this balance had broken down. Repeated bouts of malaria had worn out those children’s immune systems, and that gave EBV the chance to spread. The mosquitoes carried malaria, not the virus, and the repeated malaria played a supporting role in the cancer.

When the Body’s Guards Must Be Put to Sleep

How important this balance is can be seen clearly in organ transplant patients. When someone’s kidney or liver fails and they receive an organ from another person, the body’s immune system can treat the new organ as foreign and attack it. So the patient has to take drugs that weaken the immune system for a long time.

But the drugs do not pick and choose. They do not weaken only the guards that would attack the new organ; they weaken all the soldiers that protect the body from outside attack. EBV and the other viruses hiding in the body are always waiting for exactly this chance.

As soon as it senses the immune system weakening, EBV sets the B cells it is hiding in dividing out of control. Some patients develop a lymphoma called PTLD. This disease has been seen in up to about twenty percent of organ transplant patients.

EBV is not the only virus that has settled in most people’s bodies. Many of us carry another virus much like EBV: cytomegalovirus, or CMV. It too spreads through saliva, breast milk and other body fluids, and hides in the body for life. In developing countries, more than 90 percent of adults can carry this virus for life.

EBV and CMV are both lifelong viruses, but there is no solid evidence that CMV causes cancer. Its danger lies elsewhere: it can cause serious infections in transplant patients, and if it passes to a baby in the womb, it can cause problems up to and including deafness. Some have also linked it to ageing. The example of CMV itself shows that being able to hide in the body is not enough to cause cancer; the virus must also be able to push cells to divide.

Why Is Old Age the Age of Cancer?

Most cancers appear after the age of 50 or 60. There are two reasons, and both connect to the story above.

The first reason is the build-up of errors. Our bodies make billions of new cells every day, and each time the entire DNA has to be copied anew. Our cells are very skilled at this, but copy a book of thousands of pages countless times and a letter is bound to slip somewhere. Still, one error is not enough to turn a cell into cancer; many errors have to pile up in the same cell. And those errors take decades to pile up.

The second reason is the ageing of the guards. In our chest, in front of the heart, there is a small gland called the thymus, where new T cells are trained. From adolescence on, this training centre begins to shrink, and by old age most of it has turned into fat. Fewer new guards arrive, and the old ones grow tired.

This does not mean the immune system stops working in old age. But in everyone, the ability to control infections and recognise abnormal cells declines with age.

The best-known example of this is shingles, which we call jane khatira in Nepali. Many of us had chickenpox as children. Even after the chickenpox heals, its cause, the varicella-zoster virus, does not leave the body. It sleeps for decades in nerves near the spine. In old age, once the body’s guards have weakened, that same virus wakes up and travels along a single nerve to the skin. That is why the blisters rise in a line on only one side of the body, along the path of that nerve. Because it looks like a sacred thread (janai) worn across the chest or waist, we call it jane khatira.

Shingles is a very painful disease. Even after the blisters heal, some people feel burning in that spot for months. It can happen at any age, but the risk rises sharply after 50. By estimates, one in every three people gets shingles at some point in life. Its virus does not cause cancer, but it shows clearly how hidden viruses become active again as the body’s defences weaken with age.

In other words, in old age there are more cells with accumulated errors, and fewer guards to catch them. Whether it is a virus or an error, cancer is always waiting for the chance when the body’s soldiers grow weak.

HPV: From Warts to Cervical Cancer

When we talk about cancer-causing viruses in Nepal, the first name that comes up is human papillomavirus, or HPV. Cervical cancer is one of the most common cancers among Nepali women, and its main cause is long-term infection with HPV.

There are more than two hundred types of HPV. Most cause only ordinary problems, like warts on the hands and feet. But about a dozen types can cause cancer. Of these, HPV-16 and HPV-18 alone cause about 70 percent of cervical cancers. In men too, they can cause cancers of the throat and anus.

HPV spreads through sexual contact, and most people catch it at some point in their lives. But in most people the body clears it on its own within a year or two. Only infection that persists for a long time causes cancer, 10 to 20 years later.

In the 1970s, many scientists believed a herpes virus was the cause of cervical cancer. Harald zur Hausen in Germany suspected HPV instead, and in 1983 he found HPV-16 in cancer samples.

Once it was confirmed that HPV causes cancer, work on a vaccine against it began alongside. The first HPV vaccine arrived in 2006. The HPV vaccine lowers the future risk of cancer by preventing infection with particular cancer-causing types of the virus. It works best when given before the virus can be caught, that is, in the teenage years.

Nepal too launched a nationwide HPV vaccination campaign on February 4, 2025, World Cancer Day. More than 1.46 million girls were vaccinated: students in grades 6 to 10, and out-of-school girls aged 10 to 14. Now, every year, girls in grade 6 and out-of-school ten-year-olds receive this vaccine as part of routine immunisation.

We can hope that by the time the girls vaccinated today become mothers and grandmothers, cervical cancer in Nepal will have fallen sharply.

Hepatitis: Guests of the Liver

Hepatitis B and hepatitis C viruses stay in the liver for years. Hepatitis B passes from mother to baby at birth, and through blood and sexual contact, while hepatitis C spreads mainly through blood, through shared needles or unsafe transfusions. Decades of infection scar the liver and can lead to cirrhosis (a failing liver) and finally liver cancer.

There is an effective vaccine against hepatitis B. In Taiwan, newborns began receiving the hepatitis B vaccine as early as 1984. Ten to twelve years after the vaccination began, liver cancer among children there was found to have fallen significantly. This was the world’s first vaccine to prevent a cancer. In Nepal too, hepatitis B is part of the routine vaccines given to infants.

There is still no vaccine against hepatitis C.

Other Oncoviruses, and the Bacterium Behind “Gastric”

The AIDS epidemic introduced the world to another oncovirus. HIV is not itself an oncovirus, but it cripples the guards, the T cells. As AIDS spread in the 1980s, a skin cancer called Kaposi sarcoma and lymphomas linked to EBV shot up among patients. In 1994, a new virus hiding behind Kaposi sarcoma was found; it is now called KSHV.

It too belongs to EBV’s family, and like EBV it hides in the body for life.

The remaining two oncoviruses are rarely seen in Nepal. HTLV-1 causes a kind of blood cancer that is more common in southwestern Japan and the Caribbean. In 2008 another such virus was found behind a rare skin cancer; it is called Merkel cell polyomavirus. So EBV, KSHV, HPV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HTLV-1 and Merkel cell polyomavirus make up the seven oncoviruses we know of so far.

But it is not only viruses; bacterial infection can cause cancer too. A major cause of the burning stomach trouble we call “gastric” is a bacterium called Helicobacter pylori. By keeping the stomach inflamed and ulcerated for a long time, it can cause stomach cancer. Of all the cancers linked to infection in the world, this one accounts for the most patients.

How Did COVID Raise the Risk of Cancer?

In the last few years, almost all of us have had COVID. So another question arises: did COVID raise the risk of cancer?

The COVID virus is not itself an oncovirus, and there is no evidence that it causes cancer. But there is plenty of evidence that COVID unsettles the balance between viruses and the guards, which can smooth the way for cancer.

In severe COVID, the number of T cells in the blood drops sharply. In 2023, a study in the United States found EBV reawakened in patients with “long COVID,” who feel exhausted for a long time after COVID. Whether this reawakened EBV will cause cancer in the future, no one can say for certain yet; even after a virus wakes up, cancer can take years to appear.

The strongest signals have been seen in patients who already had cancer. A study published in Nature in 2025 showed that breast cancer cells lying dormant in the lungs of mice woke up and began to grow within days of an influenza or COVID infection. According to UK government data, among people more than five years past their cancer treatment, cancer came back after COVID and the death rate nearly doubled. Among breast cancer patients in the United States too, the chance of cancer spreading to the lungs after COVID was found to rise by more than 40 percent.

Translator’s note: in the 2025 Nature study, the UK figure comes from the UK Biobank, a research cohort rather than official government statistics, and what nearly doubled was death from cancer among long-term survivors who caught COVID.

These studies do not show more new cancers; they show a higher risk of an existing cancer coming back. That is why researchers now advise cancer survivors to protect themselves from respiratory infections and get vaccinated.

There is no reliable evidence for the claim spreading on social media that “the COVID vaccine increased cancer.” Before the hype of the algorithm, research always comes second.

Questions Still Open

We still have no full answer to why the same virus causes different cancers in different parts of the world. EBV causes cancer in children’s jaws in Africa and at the back of the nose in southern China, but in Europe it does nothing to most people. Human genes, diet and other infections may play a role. The list of viruses that hide quietly in the body and are linked to cancer is still growing.

A hundred years ago, a farmer took his chicken with a tumour to a doctor. At the time, no one believed that a virus could cause cancer. Today we know that guests like these, which slipped in as children from someone’s shared glass, are living for life in the bodies of many of us.

But this is nothing to be afraid of. Having a virus in the body and having cancer are not the same thing. Our guards have kept these guests quiet for decades, and for most people the balance lasts a lifetime. We also know what upsets that balance: transplant drugs, infections like AIDS, and old age. For some viruses, we even have vaccines that stop them from getting into the body at all. Getting the HPV and hepatitis B vaccines is the easiest and biggest thing we can do right now to protect ourselves from virus-caused cancer.

Cancer often begins on the day the body’s guards grow tired. But what do we do once the guards are tired? Can the lost guards be made strong again and given back?

Frequently asked questions

If almost everyone carries EBV, why doesn’t everyone get cancer?

T cells, the body’s guards, recognise and kill infected cells as soon as they start dividing abnormally. In most people that balance lasts a lifetime.

What is PTLD?

Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder: a lymphoma that can develop when transplant drugs weaken the immune system and EBV drives infected B cells to multiply. It is seen in up to about twenty percent of transplant patients.

Who gets the HPV vaccine in Nepal?

Since 2025, girls in grade 6 and out-of-school girls aged 10 receive it as part of routine immunisation. It works best before any exposure to the virus, in the early teens.

Does COVID cause cancer?

There is no evidence the COVID virus causes cancer itself. But severe COVID depletes T cells, and studies show a higher risk of an existing cancer coming back after infection.
